Detecting AI crawlers on this site takes three steps: record every request on
the server, check each claimed identity against what the vendor publishes,
and test behavior with honeypots. I ran that setup as a 57-day pilot, from
July 25 to September 20, 2026, and then ran a positive control to check that
the instrument works.

I asked a narrow question in three parts. Which automated clients read the
site? Are they who they claim to be? Do they follow its robots.txt? This
pilot confirmed two of its six pre-registered hypotheses and falsified two.
It also found six bugs in its own instrument, all of them silent. Then, in
September, I tested the instrument itself with a positive control. It
recorded every visit in the control (10 of 10).

Below are the method, the numbers, and what went wrong. You can trace every
figure back to the public
[experiment
log](https://github.com/Alf4kill/seotecnico.dev.br/blob/main/docs/experiment-log.md).

## How every request gets recorded and checked

This site runs Next.js on Vercel. Its proxy (Next.js middleware) records every
document request, plus every fetch of `robots.txt`, `sitemap.xml`,
`llms.txt`, and the feed, as a server-side event in a separate analytics
property. It leaves out static assets and the site's own client navigation.

Each event carries four things:

- **The claimed agent**, read from the user agent. That's a claim, not an
  identity.
- **A verification verdict**, computed only for clients that claim a known
  agent or sign the request. It's one of `verified-ip` (inside the vendor's
  published range), `verified-rdns` (forward-confirmed reverse DNS),
  `verified-signature` (a valid Web Bot Auth signature), `impersonated`
  (claims a vendor that publishes sources, and fails them), or `unverifiable`
  (the vendor publishes nothing to check against). A missing source never
  turns into an accusation.
- **Request-shape signals**, such as whether the request carries the
  `Sec-Fetch-*` headers that browsers send, and whether it's conditional.
- **A network identifier**, for correlation without identification. It's a
  salted, truncated hash of the /24 (IPv4) or /48 (IPv6) prefix, and the salt
  rotates every month. The IP address itself never leaves the server.

Two honeypot URLs measure how clients behave toward the site's rules. Each
one was designed to be reachable through exactly one channel:

- **Trap A** existed only as a `Disallow:` line in robots.txt. A client that
  fetches it has read the file and fetched what it forbids, with one caveat
  about the repository that I cover below.
- **Trap B** existed only as a link in `llms.txt`.

Before any data existed, six hypotheses went into the log, each with a
window and a falsification condition. One of them, H5, was re-specified on
September 14, 2026.

## What the pilot found

A site-wide redesign shipped on day 57 of a planned 90, so the window closed
early. I declared the early close instead of hiding it, and I report the
round as a pilot.

All told, the instrument recorded 7,208 events. My own traffic turned out to
be 24% of everything recorded, and excluding it leaves 5,480. That exclusion
is still incomplete.

| # | Hypothesis | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | A client reads robots.txt and fetches the trap it forbids | **Falsified** | Zero genuine trap hits against 1,191 robots.txt fetches. Following the `Disallow` line happens in at most 0.25% of fetches (one-sided 95%) |
| H2 | A verified training crawler fetches a disallowed page | **Confirmed** | 2026-07-30: GPTBot, `verified-ip`, three disallowed paths |
| H3 | Undeclared automation outnumbers declared-and-disallowed agents | **Confirmed** | 436 networks vs 7. **62×** by networks, 3.1× by events |
| H4 | Conditional requests reveal retrieval-purpose agents | **Falsified** | Claude-SearchBot 0/939, OAI-SearchBot 0/182, Bingbot 52/96. 93.6% of conditional requests came from undeclared clients |
| H5 | Refreshing an article's `dateModified` draws more retrieval fetches | **Inconclusive** | Underpowered by about 10×. The test needed 5 fetches of one article in 14 days, and the site gets 0.47 |
| H6 | Nothing follows links in `llms.txt` | **Supported, but underpowered** | Zero Trap B hits, but only 9 fetches of `llms.txt` in 57 days. The bound is ≤ 28% |

Three of these verdicts need their caveats spelled out.

**H2 isn't "GPTBot ignored robots.txt."** OpenAI documents GPTBot as a
training crawler, and this site's robots.txt had disallowed it since July 25,
2026. On July 30, 2026, its three requests came from inside OpenAI's
published range. But GPTBot never fetched robots.txt during the window. So
the data supports a narrower sentence. GPTBot "fetched disallowed paths
without having read the directive here." That differs from "ignored the
rule" the way a measurement differs from an accusation. Three requests on one
date prove the behavior exists, but they can't give you a rate.

**H3 is a lower bound in one direction and an upper estimate in another.**
It's computed on requests without `Sec-Fetch-*` headers, a conservative proxy
for "not a browser," so in that direction it's a lower bound. But a matcher
bug found later (bug 6 below) counted social-sharing image fetches as page
fetches. Those come mostly from link-preview bots that declare no AI agent,
so in the other direction the ratio is an upper estimate. At 62×, the
direction holds. The exact size doesn't.

**H1 and H6 carry a confound that can't be removed.** Both trap slugs sat in
tracked files in this site's public repository. The same agents crawl that
repository, so a trap hit could have come from reading it. For Trap A, the
claim still holds, because a `Disallow` line applies no matter how a client
found the URL. For Trap B, a hit wouldn't have proven that a client follows
`llms.txt`.

## Six bugs, all silent

None of these bugs threw an error. Each one turned up by reading exports by
hand or by digging into a number that looked off.

1. **The signer was never recorded.** A valid Web Bot Auth signature proves
   only that a key published at the domain named in `Signature-Agent` signed
   the request. That domain is the identity, and it never made it into the
   analytics property. When I read 28 days of data on September 13, 2026, 64
   `verified-signature` events had no signer.
2. **The current signature format was rejected.** A 2026 draft of the Web Bot
   Auth architecture changed `Signature-Agent` to a structured Dictionary, but
   my verifier accepted only the older form.
3. **Human browsing was counted as automation.** Next.js prefetches visible
   links with requests that don't ask for HTML, and those got through the
   matcher as "unknown" clients. Of the 1,041 events from my own ISP network,
   994 carry the request signature of a prefetch. Calling them prefetches is
   an inference from that signature, not an observation of each request.
4. **The public repository is itself a discovery channel.** Publishing the
   method published the bait. Git history is permanent, so I can only bound
   the confound on the two traps, never remove it.
5. **One connection produced several network identifiers.** Because the salt
   rotates monthly, my single connection showed up under three identifiers
   across the window, and a VPN exit under a fourth. The first analysis
   excluded only one of them.
6. **Social sharing images were counted as page fetches.** The matcher
   excluded `opengraph-image` only at the start of a path, so it recorded
   every nested card, like `/blog/<slug>/opengraph-image`, as a document
   fetch. It turned up while wrapping up the positive control and was fixed on
   September 25, 2026.

**Pre-registering a hypothesis without pre-registering its statistical power
gives you a test that never had a chance.** That's the lesson that applies
most broadly. Crawl volume already in the property showed in advance that H5
was ten times short. Now every hypothesis on this site states the smallest
effect its window can detect before it's registered.

## The positive control: does the instrument see a known visit?

With zero hits and no control, you can't tell "nothing came" from "the
instrument is broken." So in September, I ran a control on purpose, with 15
dated prompts, five each to ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity. Each prompt asked
the assistant to open a page and report the codes on it.

That page lived at a secret URL stored only in an environment variable,
because the assistants under test can search GitHub. It carried four codes,
one per rendering path:

- text from a Server Component
- text rendered by a `'use client'` component
- a value that existed only in the JSON-LD
- a value fetched in a `useEffect` after mount

The codes were designed to change every round, each derived from an HMAC of
the secret and round number. Rounds 01–03, however, went out with round
00's codes.

| Assistant | Opened the page | Server text | `'use client'` text | JSON-LD only | After JavaScript | Requests seen |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ChatGPT | 5 of 5 | 5 of 5 | 5 of 5 | 0 of 5 | 0 of 5 | 5, `verified-ip` |
| Claude | 5 of 5 | 5 of 5 | 5 of 5 | 0 of 5 | 0 of 5 | 5, `verified-ip` |
| Perplexity (free plan) | 0 of 5 | — | — | — | — | 0 |

**The instrument saw every visit.** Every correct answer required a request
to this server, since the codes existed nowhere else, and the proxy recorded
five verified requests per vendor. Reading that as one request per round is
an inference from the counts, and it doesn't rule out two fetches in one round
and a miss in another.
No assistant made up a code. The prompts asked for 60 in all, and whenever a
code hadn't reached an assistant, the assistant reported it as not found.

**What the assistants read is the text in the server-rendered HTML.** Output
from a `'use client'` component arrived every time, because client components
are rendered on the server too. Neither the JSON-LD nor anything that
required JavaScript ever arrived. Claude described its own tool as fetching
the HTML, extracting the text, and dropping `<script>` blocks. For the full
write-up, see
[what ChatGPT reads from a Next.js
page](/en/blog/does-chatgpt-read-javascript).
[The follow-up](/en/blog/which-ai-assistants-run-javascript) adds Gemini,
DeepSeek, and Grok, plus a round with hints.

**Perplexity, on the free plan, answered from its index.** Its user-triggered
agent made zero requests to this domain in two months, against six verified
fetches by its indexer. Asked about the home page, which the indexer had
crawled, it answered correctly without any request reaching the server. Asked
open-endedly about an article the indexer had never visited, it wrote a
confident analysis inferred from the words in its URL. It admitted it hadn't
loaded the page only after a first section that already presented that
content as the article's. In two of the conversations, the interface offered
a free preview of the paid plan's advanced search, even though the account
itself was on the free plan.

**The method had one more near-miss.** An analytics export pulled a few hours
after the rounds showed 3 and 2 requests instead of 5 and 5, because the
property hadn't finished processing the day. Taken at face value, that export
would have reported five instrument failures that never happened. Now an
export gets analyzed only after its day has been closed for 24 hours. Under
that rule, a re-export on September 27, 2026 reconfirmed the 5-and-5 figure
row by row.

## What is measured now

H14 runs from September 25 to December 24, 2026. It asks whether anything
requests Markdown from this site with `Accept: text/markdown`. It only
measures, and every response stays HTML. Markdown serving will only be built
if at least five distinct networks or two verified agents ask for it.

Version 2 of the detection instrument is registered, not built. Its
hypotheses, H7–H13, went into the log before any of its code. Its design would
add a coherence vector over request headers, new discovery channels whose slugs
never enter a tracked file, and a repository-only canary that would measure
how often the public repository itself is crawled for URLs. None of it is
built yet, so none of it measures anything. The one exception is H11, which
was computed from pilot data.
