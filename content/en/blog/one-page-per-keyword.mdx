Building one page per keyword, with a URL for each way of searching for the
same product, is a model I wouldn't recommend to a company today. Three
passages in Google's spam policies describe its pieces. Google's guide on
optimizing for generative AI features also says its systems understand
synonyms without a page for each phrasing.

This model doesn't have to fail to be a bad bet. Going by what Google itself
documents, the part that can bring search traffic doesn't depend on the part
the policy names.

My earlier article, [Scaled content abuse](/en/blog/scaled-content-abuse),
shows what the policy says, using a fictional site. This one measures a real
site built on that model. I read the HTML its server sent on November 10,
2026, piece by piece, and set each piece beside Google's text.

I don't identify the site. I don't have its Search Console, and I don't use
search results. What you'll find here is what the server sends, what the
documentation says, and the probable outcome. I don't know what Google
actually did with this site.

## The site and how I measured it

The site belongs to an industrial equipment maker. It's Brazilian and written
in Portuguese. Its HTML sitemap page lists about 190 URLs. About 180 of them
come from the same template, one per keyword variation, and about ten are
institutional or index pages. All of them answered 200.

I measured it with [a script in this
repository](https://github.com/Alf4kill/seotecnico.dev.br/blob/main/scripts/template-anatomy.mjs).
It sends one request per second, reads `robots.txt` first, and uses a
User-Agent that identifies it. It reads raw HTML only and doesn't run
JavaScript or search Google.

As a control, I ran the same script in the same round on the 25 Portuguese
articles on seotecnico.dev.br, the site you're reading. That control isn't a
competitor. It shows what the method returns on a set of pages with no
template, so you have a reference point for judging whether a number from the
case is big or small.

## Each way of asking for the same thing gets its own URL

The template's URLs sit at the domain root and follow a grammar: an
optional commercial prefix, then a product, a modifier, and an application. In
an invented sector, they'd look like this.

```text
/esteira-transportadora
/esteira-transportadora-industrial
/esteira-transportadora-mineracao
/fabricante-esteira-transportadora
/empresa-esteira-transportadora-industrial
```

That block is illustrative. I invented its sector and URLs, and I wrote its
slugs in Portuguese, where "fabricante" means maker, "empresa" means company,
and "esteira transportadora" means conveyor belt. The grammar and numbers
below come from the measured site.

| Measure | Measured site | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Pairs of URLs sharing two thirds or more of their terms | about 80 pairs, 46% of the pages | 0 |
| URLs that start with a commercial-intent word (maker, supplier, company, best) | 17% | 0 |

In my reading, a commercial prefix doesn't change what's being asked for. The
product page already answers anyone looking for who makes it.

Google's spam policies describe doorways as pages created to rank for
specific, similar searches. Their examples include substantially similar pages
that are closer to search results than to a browseable hierarchy.

Google's generative AI guide says that creating separate content for every
variation of how people search, primarily to manipulate rankings, violates the
scaled content abuse policy. It also says a high number of pages doesn't make
a site more relevant.

## Rewritten text avoids deduplication but still fits the policy's description

My earlier article describes a template where only the keyword changes. A
documented mechanism covers that case. Google identifies each page's primary
content, clusters pages whose content is very similar, and indexes one of them
as the canonical.

The measured site is the other version of the model, where every page has
rewritten text. I measured literal similarity between each page and the page
most like it, and the median is 0.068. No pair reaches 0.30. A duplicate text
checker would tell you everything is fine, and the documented clustering of
near-identical pages isn't what you'd expect here.

You can still see the template in other measures.

| Measure | Measured site | Control (25 articles) |
|---|---|---|
| Words in the page's own text (median) | about 750 | 2,722 |
| Literal similarity with the most similar page (median) | 0.068 | 0.011 |
| Pairs with literal similarity of 0.30 or more | 0 | 0 |
| Sentences that repeat in 10% of the texts or more | 10 | 3 |
| Texts with at least one of those sentences | 89% | 100% |
| Pages that open with the same six words | 42% | no repeated opening |
| Texts with no number followed by a unit of measure | 100% | 56% |

To count sentences, the script replaces each page's keyword with a
placeholder, so the same sentence with another product's name counts as one.
The most repeated sentence appears on more than half of the pages. It
recommends another of the company's products by name and has no link. In the
control, the only sentence present in all 25 texts is the author line that
closes each article.

Read the last row carefully. On an equipment page, capacity, power, or
temperature is the content you'd expect, and none of the texts has such a
number. Fewer than one in ten pages carries a percentage, and none carries a
quantity with a unit. In the control, the same count is 14 of 25, because an
article about hreflang has no unit to quote. This measure only means something
when you compare it with what the page is supposed to describe.

Here are the same two paths as a table.

| Set of pages | What to expect |
|---|---|
| Nearly identical text, only the keyword changes | Clustering: one page becomes canonical and the others are crawled less often (documented) |
| Rewritten text, as on the measured site | No clustering as copies, and the set is what the scaled content policy describes (my reading) |

Google's scaled content abuse policy describes many pages generated for the
primary purpose of manipulating rankings, with little value for the reader, no
matter how they're produced. One of its examples is transformation by swapping
in synonyms. That example is about content scraped from elsewhere. Applying it
to a site's own template is my reading, not Google's sentence.

One caveat about method. Vocabulary similarity is often used to "prove"
rewriting, and it's weak evidence. On the measured site, it's 0.667 with plain
term frequency and 0.340 when terms common to the whole set weigh less. My
control, a single-niche blog with no template, gives 0.500 and 0.235. That
number tracks the subject as much as it tracks the template.

## Every page carries the same city list

Every template page carries the same "regionalization section," a list of
cities from all over Brazil in 26 tabs, one per state. This city list is
identical on every page, and it has more than five thousand names.

|---|---|

Google's spam policies give this pattern as a keyword stuffing example. They
mention blocks of text that list the cities and regions a page is trying to
rank for. It's the literal example.

Tabs aren't the problem. That same policies page lists tabbed and accordion
content among elements that don't violate the hidden text policy.

Size isn't the problem either. Googlebot reads the first 2 MB of an HTML file,
and my control's articles have a median size of 288 KB. This measurement is
about proportion. More than half of every page is that same list, and a page's
own text is 5.8% of what it says.

This block's probable purpose is to get these pages into searches that include
a city name, and for those searches the documentation points elsewhere. Business
Profile help describes local results as based mainly on relevance of the
profile, distance, and prominence, and it caps service areas at 20. A list of
names on your page doesn't change your distance to the searcher. I didn't
measure whether the list brings any impressions for a product-plus-city
search.

## Title, H1, alt, and description all come from the keyword

This template fills four fields with the keyword. The documentation has a
passage against only one of them.

| Field | On the measured site | What the documentation says |
|---|---|---|
| `<title>` equal to the H1, plus the brand | all of them, all distinct | Not a problem. The title links guide suggests making the main title clear, for example in the first visible `<h1>`. The control does the same on 25 of 25 |
| Every image's `alt` equal to the H1 | all of them | The image guide asks for `alt` text that describes the image and refers to filling it with keywords as keyword stuffing |
| Description equal to the text's opening, cut off with an ellipsis | all of them, all distinct | Generating the description by rule is appropriate when it is specific to the page |
| Meta keywords, the same value across the whole site | all pages | Not used by Search |

You can see any page's title, description, and H1 side by side in the [meta
tag validator](/en/tools/meta-tag-validator).

## Every page links to every other page

Each template page links to 100% of the others, in a fixed block. That comes
to a median of about 190 internal links per page. In the control, each article
links to 21% of the others.

Linking this way guarantees discovery, because any page is one link from any
other. It also means the links can no longer say what matters. Every template
page gets the same links from the same block, and the site lacks the
browseable hierarchy that the doorway example uses as its contrast. That's my
reading. No document says a flat mesh is a problem in itself.

Two details show where link choice was left to the template. None of the texts
contains an in-text link to another page on the site. In the control, 24 of 25
articles do. The only block that chooses per page, "related pages," carries 4
links. It never recommends 22% of the pages, and it recommends 8% of them more
than ten times.

## An empty sitemap, no canonical, and three URL variants answering 200

Here are the signals I checked, next to the control's.

| Signal | Measured site | Control |
|---|---|---|
| `sitemap.xml` | answers 200, with 0 URLs | 80 URLs |
| Pages with `rel="canonical"` | none | 25 of 25, self-referencing |
| One template page with a trailing slash and with `?x=1`, and the home page at `/index.php` | 200 for all three, no redirect | not tested |
| JSON-LD on the template pages | `BreadcrumbList` only | `Article`, `BreadcrumbList`, `FAQPage` |
| Institutional pages sharing their `<title>` with another page | 8 in 10 | not applicable |
| Invented URL | 302 to a page that answers 404 | 404 |

According to the documentation, none of this is an error. Google says you may
not need a sitemap when your site is small, up to about 500 pages, or when
it's fully linked internally. It also says no canonicalization method is
required. If you don't indicate a preference, Google picks the version it
considers best. The invented URL ends in a real 404, as the measurement in my
[soft 404 article](/en/blog/soft-404) shows.

What these signals share is that the site declares nothing. The page I tested
answers at three URLs, and none of them points to a preferred version. A
sitemap would be a weak signal in favor of one of them, and this one is empty.
That leaves the choice entirely to Google. Identity works the same way,
because no structured data says which organization this is. For the repeated
titles, the title links guide warns that Google may build another title from
the page.

## What the site does that is not a problem

An analysis like this one easily turns into a list of faults. These facts
showed up in the measurement, and the documentation has nothing against them.

| Fact | Why it is not a finding |
|---|---|
| Title equal to the H1 | The title links guide suggests it, and the control does the same |
| Content inside tabs | Tabs and accordions are allowed |
| Description generated by rule | Appropriate when it is specific to the page |
| No `rel="canonical"` | Not required |
| An unused sitemap on a small, fully linked site | May not be needed |
| About 470 KB of HTML | Below the 2 MB limit |
| Content present without JavaScript | What the server sends is what there is |
| Having many landing pages | The policy is about purpose and value, not a count |

## Estimating the probable outcome, piece by piece

This table sets each piece beside the passage that describes it.

| Piece | Measured | Where Google describes it | Probable outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| One URL per variation | about 80 pairs of nearly identical URLs | Doorway and the generative AI guide | Several pages for one search. Systems that understand synonyms do not need them |
| Text rewritten from a template | 10 template sentences in 89% of the texts | Scaled content abuse | Outside the clustering of copies, inside the policy's description |
| Nationwide city list | 55% of the HTML, identical on every page | Keyword stuffing, the literal example | The piece with the most direct match |
| `alt` equal to the keyword | every page | Image guide | The `alt` describes no image |
| Flat link mesh | 100% of the other pages | No passage against it | Everything is discovered, and nothing is singled out |
| No canonical, variants answering 200 | no page with a canonical | Canonicalization | Google picks each page's URL |

For the first three pieces, the documented consequence is the general one in
the spam policies. Sites that violate the policies may rank lower or not
appear in results. Google detects violations with automated systems and, as
needed, human review that can result in a manual action. My table says the
site matches descriptions. It doesn't say any of those consequences happened.

Some things you can't know from the outside: how many of these pages are in
the index, whether any of them brings clicks, whether there's a manual action,
and whether the city list generates any impressions. All of that is in the
site's Search Console, which I don't have.

So my recommendation isn't "this doesn't work." It's a risk calculation. What
the model can gain is showing up for variations of the same product, and
Google itself says you don't need a page per variation for that. Its exposure
comes from the pieces added to multiply pages: variations with no real
difference, template text, and the city list. Remove those pieces and you lose
nothing that could bring search traffic.

## What I would do instead

These recommendations are conditional, and none of them promises traffic.
Google's page on core updates says improvements can take months to be
recognized and that there's no guarantee.

- **One page per thing that exists.** If your company has five product lines,
  that's five pages. Each one carries what only that page can say, like
  capacity, consumption, material, and an installation photo. Redirect
  spelling variations to each line's main page.
- **Coverage stated where it's true.** If regional service matters to you,
  write a page about where you serve, with the real places. Then take the list
  of thousands of cities off the other pages.
- **Links that choose.** Link from line to model and from model to
  application, and link inside the text when it mentions another page.
- **Declared signals.** Use a self-referencing canonical, one URL per page
  with redirects from its variants, a sitemap your system generates, and
  organization data on your home page.

## Where the control's numbers come from in Next.js

My [technical SEO guide for Next.js](/en/guide/technical-seo-nextjs) uses this
site as its reference implementation, and the control above is this site. I
ran the same script on its 25 Portuguese articles, and three pieces of its
code explain the result.

First, the route list comes from written files, not from a keyword list.
That's why the control has zero pairs of nearly identical URLs.

```tsx
// src/app/(pt)/blog/[slug]/page.tsx, linhas 17 a 22
// Somente slugs existentes em /content/blog são gerados; o resto é 404.
export const dynamicParams = false

export function generateStaticParams() {
  return getAllPosts().map(({ frontmatter }) => ({ slug: frontmatter.slug }))
}
```

Second, every page declares its own canonical, because the metadata helper
derives it from the path. That's 25 of 25 in the control.

```ts
// src/lib/metadata.ts, linhas 151 a 155
  return {
    title: absoluteTitle ? { absolute: title } : title,
    description,
    alternates: {
      canonical: path,
```

Third, the sitemap is built from the same content, so there's no manual step
to forget.

```ts
// src/app/sitemap.ts, linhas 94 a 100
  const postPages: MetadataRoute.Sitemap = posts.map(({ frontmatter }) => ({
    url: `${base}/blog/${frontmatter.slug}`,
    lastModified: toDate(frontmatter.dateModified),
    changeFrequency: 'monthly',
    priority: 0.7,
    images: postImages[frontmatter.slug]?.map((path) => `${base}${path}`),
  }))
```

Next.js doesn't protect you from the model. Feed `generateStaticParams` a list
of variations and you get the same pages, with canonical and sitemap in order.
What differs is what the list represents. For details on each piece,
read [redirects and canonicals in
Next.js](/en/blog/nextjs-redirects-canonicals) and [dynamic sitemap in
Next.js](/en/blog/nextjs-dynamic-sitemap).

To measure your own site, give the script a targets file and run it in two
phases.

```bash
node scripts/template-anatomy.mjs fetch --targets audits/case-studies/template-anatomy.example.json --only SITE
node scripts/template-anatomy.mjs analyze --targets audits/case-studies/template-anatomy.example.json --only SITE
```

## Method and limits

- **Collection.** I ran the round on November 10, 2026 (the UTC date, which
  was the evening of November 9 in Brasília time). The script used a
  User-Agent that identifies it, respected `robots.txt`, and sent one request
  per second. In the [versioned
  summary](https://github.com/Alf4kill/seotecnico.dev.br/blob/main/audits/case-studies/template-anatomy-2026-11-10/summary.json),
  you'll find only a code and numbers. I don't publish the site's address, the
  downloaded HTML, or the sentences.
- **Rounded totals.** I rounded the case's totals and give its page counts as
  proportions so the site can't be identified. The versioned summary follows
  the same rule. The control's numbers are exact.
- **Definitions.** Literal similarity is the Jaccard similarity of five-word
  sequences between a page's text and its most similar page's text. A page's
  own text runs from where its article starts to the contact call to action,
  without the city list. You can read the unit-of-measure list in the script.
- **Heuristics.** My text measures describe the set, and none of them is a
  verdict on a page. As the control shows, the unit-of-measure one depends on
  the kind of page.
- **Not measured.** Indexing, clicks, positions, and manual actions, because I
  analyzed the site from the outside. I ran no Google searches for this
  article. Measuring the city list's effect on searches with a city name needs
  manual tests that I didn't run.
- **Rendering.** The script doesn't run JavaScript. Everything I measured
  (text, list, links, and metadata) was in the HTML the server sent.

I analyzed this public site from the outside, only to study the method. I
don't identify it, I have no relationship with it, and it didn't take part.
This isn't an audit, a recommendation to its owner, or a verdict on it. Nobody
sponsored this analysis, and I don't sell any service based on it.
