llms.txt is a proposed Markdown file, served from the site root
(`/llms.txt`), that summarizes the site and lists the content language models
should read. On this site, between July 25 and September 20, 2026, the file
was fetched 9 times, and no client (browser, crawler or agent) followed the
link that existed only inside it. That doesn't prove llms.txt is useless. It
proves that, here, almost nothing reads it.

For Google Search, there's already an official answer: Google ignores the
file. The rest of this article covers what a small site can measure on its
own, where my test fell short and what I'm measuring now.

## What llms.txt is

Jeremy Howard published the proposal on September 3, 2024, at
[llmstxt.org](https://llmstxt.org/). The format is deliberately simple: an H1
with the site's name (the only required part), a summary in a blockquote,
optional paragraphs, and H2 sections containing lists of Markdown links. A
section named `Optional` marks what can be dropped when the model's context
window is tight.

Two details of the proposal tend to get lost in summaries. First, the stated
target is inference, the moment a model answers someone, not training.
Second, the proposal also suggests serving a Markdown version of each page at
the same address, with `.md` appended or replacing the extension. That second
idea comes back later, in H14.

llms.txt isn't a directive. robots.txt says what a crawler may crawl;
llms.txt only suggests what to read. Listing a URL in it doesn't grant access,
and leaving a URL out doesn't protect it.

### What the vendors say

Google has a position on record. Its [official guide to optimizing for
generative AI features in
Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide),
updated on July 10, 2026, says you don't need to create AI text files,
Markdown or new markup to appear in Search, because Google Search ignores
them. Keeping such a file for other services, the same guide says, neither
helps nor hurts your visibility in Search.

For OpenAI and Anthropic, I checked their official crawler pages in October
2026 and found nothing about reading other sites' llms.txt. OpenAI's
documentation publishes its own llms.txt, which shows it uses the format as a
publisher, not as a reader. The lack of documentation doesn't prove they
don't read it; it's simply what's on the record.

## How I tested it: a link that exists only in llms.txt

Counting fetches of the file is easy: filter requests to `/llms.txt`. The hard
part is knowing whether anyone uses what they read. To find out, during the
crawler detection pilot I ran on this site ([the full case
study](/en/case-studies/ai-crawler-detection)), I added a link in llms.txt to
a URL that existed nowhere else on the site: no page linked to it and it
wasn't in the sitemap. I called that URL Trap B.

The logic is simple. If a client reads llms.txt and follows its links, sooner
or later it hits the trap. If the trap is never hit, nobody followed the
file, at least not through that link.

The pilot was planned for 90 days. I closed it at 57, running from July 25 to
September 20, 2026, when the site's redesign was deployed. The early close and
the reason for it are documented in the pilot's write-up.

## The result: 9 fetches, 0 clicks

| Measure (Jul 25 to Sep 20, 2026) | Count |
|---|---|
| Fetches of `/llms.txt` | 9 |
| Hits on Trap B (link only in llms.txt) | 0 |
| Fetches of `/robots.txt`, same period | 1,191 |

The robots.txt comparison is only an order-of-magnitude check: over the same
period, the access-rules file was fetched 1,191 times and llms.txt 9 times.
That's a difference of two orders of magnitude in how much interest clients
showed in the two files.

Zero out of nine looks conclusive, but it isn't. With zero hits in n fetches,
the one-sided 95% upper bound on the true follow rate is `1 − 0.05^(1/n)`.
For n = 9, that comes to 28%. Put simply, the result is consistent with up
to 28% of fetches following the link. The verdict I recorded for the
hypothesis was "supported, but the bound is useless": with nine fetches,
"nothing follows llms.txt" can't be told apart from "almost nothing reads
it". The real finding is the low exposure, not the zero.

### Who fetched it

The pilot's write-up doesn't identify who fetched the file, and I won't name
anyone here either. There is a partial picture: in an interim 28-day pull,
August 16 to September 12, 2026, excluding traffic from my own network, the
file was fetched 5 times, all by clients that did **not** identify themselves
as AI agents, and none by a declared AI agent.

That bears on a separate prediction, registered the day the file went live:
**no fetch of `/llms.txt` by a major vendor's AI agent within 90 days**. The
window closes on October 23, 2026, and the verdict will be added to this
article when it does. Until then, the 5 fetches in that slice are an interim
observation, not a result.

## Why the test isn't conclusive

There are two problems, and the second is worse.

**Low statistical power.** Nine fetches isn't much. A quick calculation shows
the scale of the problem: if 1 in 10 readers followed the link, the chance of
still seeing zero hits in 9 fetches would be 0.9⁹, about 39%. Even with a
real effect, the test had almost a four-in-ten chance of coming back empty.

**A confound from the public repository.** The trap's address was in a
versioned file in the site's repository, which is public on GitHub. Any hit
on the trap could have come from someone (or some bot) reading the repository
rather than llms.txt. So even a hit on the trap wouldn't have proved that
anyone follows llms.txt. The design could only say something if it returned
zero, and zero, with 9 fetches, says very little.

That mistake was mine, and I'm sharing it because it's an easy one to repeat
when setting up this kind of test in an open-source project: the file you use
to configure the trap is itself a discovery channel.

The lesson I recorded when closing the pilot was to pre-register statistical
power, not just the hypothesis. Another hypothesis in the same pilot, H5, made
the problem obvious at closing: it needed roughly ten times as many crawler
visits as the site got, and no extension of the window would fix that. Since
then, every hypothesis registered here states, next to its window, the
smallest effect it can detect.

## What came next: asking for Markdown with `Accept: text/markdown`

The proposal's second idea, serving Markdown instead of HTML, has a variant
that needs no new URLs: content negotiation. The client sends an `Accept:
text/markdown` header and the server picks the response format. Cloudflare
documents exactly this mechanism in its [Markdown for
Agents](https://developers.cloudflare.com/fundamentals/reference/markdown-for-agents/)
feature, which converts HTML to Markdown at the edge when the client asks
for it.

Before building anything, I want to know whether anyone asks. H14 runs from
September 25 to December 24, 2026 and only measures: every response stays
HTML. The hypothesis is that at least one genuine request asks for a page in
Markdown during the window. Based on the pilot's volume, the window should
see around 2,700 requests that don't come from a browser, so a zero would cap
the share asking for Markdown at about 0.1% of them. The decision rule was
published before the window opened: Markdown serving will only be built if
the requests come from at least 5 distinct networks or from 2 agents with
verified identity.

To see what your own server does when a client asks for Markdown:

```bash
# illustrative: replace with your domain
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{content_type}\n" \
  -H "Accept: text/markdown" \
  https://your-domain.com/
```

If it returns `text/html`, your server ignores the preference, which is the
default for most sites, and for this one during H14.

Alongside this, the second version of the detection instrument, with
hypotheses H7 to H13 pre-registered, plans discovery channels whose addresses
never go into a versioned file, plus a trap that exists only in the
repository, to measure how big the leak is. That's the direct fix for the
Trap B confound. It's registered but not built yet.

## Is it worth creating an llms.txt?

The honest answer depends on your situation.

- **If code can generate the file** from something the site already has, like
  the post index, keeping it costs next to nothing. Creating it does no harm:
  Google itself says the file neither helps nor hurts in Search.
- **If it needs manual upkeep**, it goes stale. A hand-written llms.txt that
  nobody maintains points to URLs that have changed or disappeared, and
  anything that follows it gets a wrong map of your site.
- **If the goal is to show up in AI answers**, the file isn't where to put
  your effort. When I tested five assistants between September and October
  2026, their answers drew on the text of the served HTML of whatever page
  they opened ([the experiment, with dates, plans and
  conditions](/en/blog/which-ai-assistants-run-javascript)). The test neither
  offered nor measured llms.txt; what it shows is that HTML is enough to get
  read. To check what that HTML puts in the `<head>`, the [meta tag
  validator](/en/tools/meta-tag-validator) reads the same raw server response.

This site still serves the file. Code generates it and it's part of the
measurement instrument, so keeping it costs little and it still produces
data.

## In Next.js: how this site generates the file

This site's llms.txt is built by `src/lib/llms-txt.ts`, from the same source as
the sitemap: the guide and the list of posts read from `/content`. The file's
header reuses `site.description`, the site's canonical description, instead
of separately written text, so if the description changes, llms.txt changes
with it.

```ts
// src/lib/llms-txt.ts (lines 71–79)
export function buildLlmsTxt(guide: Post, posts: Post[], guideEn?: Post, postsEn: Post[] = []): string {
  const sections = [
    `# ${site.name}`,
    '',
    `> ${site.description} Conteúdo em português (pt-BR), escrito por ${site.author.name}. Cada técnica documentada aqui está implementada neste próprio domínio e medida com Google Search Console, CrUX e experimentos antes/depois — nada é publicado antes de ser implementado e medido.`,
    '',
    '## Guia (pilar)',
    '',
    linkLine('/guia/seo-tecnico-nextjs', guide.frontmatter.title, guide.frontmatter.description),
```

The file is written in Portuguese, the site's primary language. The
blockquote says the content is in Portuguese, written by the author, and that
every technique documented is implemented on this domain and measured before
it's published.

A static route handler, `src/app/llms.txt/route.ts`, serves the file. Like
`feed.xml`, it's built at deploy time: new content arrives by commit, and
every commit already triggers a deploy.

```ts
// src/app/llms.txt/route.ts (lines 6–12)
export const dynamic = 'force-static'

export function GET() {
  return new Response(buildLlmsTxt(getGuide(), getAllPosts(), getGuide('en'), getAllEnglishPosts()), {
    headers: { 'Content-Type': 'text/plain; charset=utf-8' },
  })
}
```

An excerpt of the file appears in the Portuguese version of this article. It
deliberately stops at the first block of links: further down, the file
contains the trap's section, and reproducing it would open a new path to the
trap. For the same reason, neither version links to the file itself, since
every human visit from here would be added to the fetch count the prediction
above is still measuring.

For the rest of this site's reference implementation, see the [technical SEO
guide for Next.js](/en/guide/technical-seo-nextjs).

## How to check whether anyone reads your llms.txt

**1. Confirm the file responds.**

```bash
# illustrative: replace with your domain
curl -sI https://your-domain.com/llms.txt
```

You want a `200` status and a text `content-type`. If you get a 404, the rest
is just theory.

**2. Count the fetches in your access log** by filtering for the `/llms.txt`
path, and compare with `/robots.txt` over the same period. That comparison
gives you the order of magnitude without needing a share of all requests.
Filter out your own requests: in the pilot, traffic from my own network made
up 24% of everything recorded.

**3. Be careful how you name agents.** What your log contains is the user
agent the request claims. Without verifying identity (vendor-published IP
ranges, reverse DNS), the accurate sentence is "requests identifying
themselves as X", not "X read my file". The [case
study](/en/case-studies/ai-crawler-detection) shows how I did that
verification.

**4. To find out whether anyone follows the file, use a trap link**: a URL
that exists only in llms.txt. And learn from my mistake: the address can't
appear in a versioned file in a public repository, in the sitemap, or on any
page.

**5. Before you start, work out how many fetches you'll need.** With zero
hits, the 95% upper bound is `1 − 0.05^(1/n)`:

| Fetches of llms.txt (n) | Upper bound with 0 hits |
|---|---|
| 9 | 28% |
| 30 | 9.5% |
| 59 | 5% |
| 100 | 3% |

At the pilot's rate (9 fetches in 57 days), getting to 59 fetches would take
just over a year. On a small site, "does anyone follow my llms.txt?" probably
won't get a solid answer within a few months. Better to know that before you
start than to find out at the end, as I did.
