On this site, hardly at all, and Google Search ignores it. llms.txt is a
proposed Markdown file, served at the site root (`/llms.txt`), that
summarizes a site and lists the content language models should read.
Between July 25 and September 20, 2026, mine was fetched 9 times, and no
client (browser, crawler, or agent) followed the link that existed only
inside it.

That doesn't prove llms.txt is useless. It proves that almost nothing
fetches it here. For Google Search, an official answer already exists:
Google ignores the file. The rest of this article covers what a small site
can measure on its own, where my test failed, and what I'm measuring now.

## What llms.txt is

Jeremy Howard published the proposal on September 3, 2024, at
[llmstxt.org](https://llmstxt.org/). He kept the format deliberately
simple:

- an H1 with the site's name, which is the only required part
- a summary in a blockquote
- optional paragraphs
- H2 sections with lists of Markdown links

A section named `Optional` marks what a model can drop when it's short on
context.

Summaries of the proposal, at least in Portuguese, tend to leave out two
details. First, its stated
target is inference (the moment a model answers someone), not training.
Second, it also suggests serving a Markdown version of each page at the
same address, with `.md` appended or replacing the extension. That second
idea comes back later, as hypothesis H14.

llms.txt isn't a directive. robots.txt tells a crawler what it may crawl,
while llms.txt only suggests what to read. Listing a URL in it doesn't
grant access, and leaving a URL out doesn't protect it.

### What the vendors say

Google has a written position. Its
[official guide to optimizing for generative AI features in
Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide),
updated July 10, 2026, says you don't need to create AI text files,
Markdown, or new markup to appear in Search, because Google Search ignores
them. According to the same guide, keeping such a file for other services
neither helps nor hurts your visibility in Search.

For OpenAI and Anthropic, I found nothing. In October 2026, I checked both
companies' official pages about their crawlers and found no mention of
reading llms.txt from third-party sites. OpenAI's documentation does publish
its own llms.txt, which shows OpenAI using the format as a publisher, not as
a reader. Missing documentation doesn't prove they don't read it. It only
tells you what's written down.

## How I tested it: a link that exists only in llms.txt

Counting fetches is easy. You filter requests to `/llms.txt`. Knowing
whether anyone uses what they fetched is the hard part.

For the
[crawler detection pilot I ran on this
site](/en/case-studies/ai-crawler-detection),
I put a link in llms.txt to a URL that existed nowhere else on the site. No
page linked to it, and it wasn't in the sitemap. I called that URL Trap B.
The pilot's write-up has the full method and all six hypotheses.

If a client fetches llms.txt and follows its links, it hits the trap sooner
or later. If nobody ever hits the trap, nobody followed the file, at least
not through that link.

I planned the pilot for 90 days and closed it after 57, when I deployed the
site redesign. It ran from July 25 to September 20, 2026, and its write-up
states that it closed early and why.

## The result: 9 fetches, 0 hits

| Measure (Jul 25 to Sep 20, 2026) | Count |
|---|---|
| Fetches of `/llms.txt` | 9 |
| Hits on Trap B (link only in llms.txt) | 0 |
| Fetches of `/robots.txt`, same period | 1,191 |

Read the robots.txt row as an order of magnitude and nothing more. Over the
same period, clients fetched the access-rules file 1,191 times and llms.txt
9 times. That's a gap of two orders of magnitude in how interested clients
were in each file.

Zero out of nine looks conclusive. It isn't. With zero hits in n fetches,
the one-sided 95% upper bound on the true follow rate is `1 − 0.05^(1/n)`.
For n = 9, that comes to 28%, so the result is compatible with up to 28% of
fetches following the link. I logged the verdict for this hypothesis as
"supported, but the bound is useless." With nine fetches, you can't tell
"nothing follows llms.txt" apart from "almost nothing reads it." What the
test found is how little exposure the file got, not the zero.

### Who fetched it

The pilot's write-up doesn't identify who fetched the file, and I won't
name anyone here either. An interim 28-day pull from August 16 to September
12, 2026, with my own network's traffic excluded, gives a partial picture.
In that slice, the file was fetched 5 times, all by clients that did
**not** identify themselves as AI agents. No declared AI agent fetched it.

That ties to a separate prediction I pre-registered the day the file went
live: **no fetch of `/llms.txt` by a major vendor's AI agent within 90
days**. Its window closes October 23, 2026, and I'll add the verdict to this
article when it does. Until then, those 5 fetches are an interim
observation, not a result.

## Why the test isn't conclusive

My design had two limits, and the second is worse than the first.

**Low statistical power.** Nine fetches isn't many. If 1 in 10 readers
followed the link, a simple calculation puts the chance of still seeing
zero hits in 9 fetches at 0.9⁹, about 39%. So even with a real effect, the
test had nearly a 4 in 10 chance of coming back zero.

**A confound from the public repository.** The site's repository is public
on GitHub, and the trap's address sat in a versioned file there. Any hit on
the trap could have come from someone, or some bot, reading the repository
rather than llms.txt. So even a hit wouldn't have proved that anyone
follows llms.txt. Only a zero could tell me anything, and a zero from 9
fetches says little.

That mistake was mine, and I'm describing it because anyone setting up
this kind of test in an open-source project can make it too. The file you
configure the trap in is itself a discovery channel.

When I closed the pilot, the lesson I recorded was to pre-register the
power, not just the hypothesis. H5, another hypothesis in that pilot, showed the
problem at closing. It needed roughly ten times as many crawler visits as
the site got, and no longer window would have fixed that. Since then, every
hypothesis I pre-register here states, next to its window, the smallest
effect it can detect.

## What came next: asking for Markdown with `Accept: text/markdown`

There's a variant of the proposal's second idea, serving Markdown instead
of HTML, that needs no new URLs. It's called content negotiation. A client
sends the
`Accept: text/markdown` header, and the server decides what format to
respond with. Cloudflare documents exactly this mechanism in its
[Markdown for
Agents](https://developers.cloudflare.com/fundamentals/reference/markdown-for-agents/)
feature, which converts HTML to Markdown at the edge when the client asks
for it.

Before building anything, I want to know whether anyone asks. H14 runs from
September 25 to December 24, 2026, and only measures. Every response stays
HTML. It predicts that at least one real request asks for a page in
Markdown within the window. At the pilot's volume, the window should bring
close to 2,700 requests that don't come from a browser, so a zero would cap
the share asking for Markdown at about 0.1% of them.

I published the decision rule before H14 started. I'll build Markdown
serving only if those requests come from at least 5 distinct networks or
from 2 agents with verified identity.

To see what your own server does when a client asks for Markdown, run this:

```bash
# illustrative: replace with your domain
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{content_type}\n" \
  -H "Accept: text/markdown" \
  https://your-domain.com/
```

If you get `text/html` back, your server ignores the preference. That's the
default behavior of most sites, and of this one during H14.

I've also pre-registered a second version of the detection instrument, with
hypotheses H7 to H13. Its design calls for discovery channels whose addresses
never go into a versioned file, plus a trap that exists only in the
repository, to measure how big the leak is. That's the direct fix for the
Trap B confound. It's pre-registered but not built yet.

## Is it worth creating an llms.txt?

Honestly, it depends.

- **If code can generate the file** from something your site already has,
  such as its post index, keeping it costs close to nothing. Creating it
  does no harm either. Google itself says the file neither helps nor hurts
  in Search.
- **If you have to maintain it by hand**, it goes stale. A forgotten
  hand-written llms.txt points to URLs that have changed or vanished, and
  anyone who follows it gets a wrong map of the site.
- **If your goal is to appear in AI answers**, put your effort somewhere
  else. When I tested five assistants between September and October 2026,
  their answers carried text from the served HTML of the page they opened
  ([the experiment, with dates, plans, and conditions](/en/blog/which-ai-assistants-run-javascript)).
  That test didn't offer or measure llms.txt. It does show that the HTML is
  enough to get read. To check what your HTML delivers in the `<head>`, use
  the [meta tag validator](/en/tools/meta-tag-validator), which reads the
  same raw server response.

I keep serving this site's file. Code generates it, and it's part of the
measurement instrument, so it costs little to keep and still produces
data.

## In Next.js: how this site generates the file

`src/lib/llms-txt.ts` builds this site's llms.txt from the same source as
the sitemap, which is the guide plus the list of posts read from
`/content`.
Its header reuses `site.description`, the site's central description,
instead of separate text, so when that description changes, llms.txt
follows.

```ts
// src/lib/llms-txt.ts (lines 71–79)
export function buildLlmsTxt(guide: Post, posts: Post[], guideEn?: Post, postsEn: Post[] = []): string {
  const sections = [
    `# ${site.name}`,
    '',
    `> ${site.description} Conteúdo em português (pt-BR), escrito por ${site.author.name}. Cada técnica documentada aqui está implementada neste próprio domínio e medida com Google Search Console, CrUX e experimentos antes/depois — nada é publicado antes de ser implementado e medido.`,
    '',
    '## Guia (pilar)',
    '',
    linkLine('/guia/seo-tecnico-nextjs', guide.frontmatter.title, guide.frontmatter.description),
```

llms.txt itself is in Portuguese, the site's primary language. Its
blockquote says the content is in Portuguese and written by the author,
and that every technique it documents is implemented on this domain and
measured before it's published.

A static route handler, `src/app/llms.txt/route.ts`, serves the file. Like
`feed.xml`, it's generated at build time. New content arrives by commit,
and every commit already triggers a deploy.

```ts
// src/app/llms.txt/route.ts (lines 6–12)
export const dynamic = 'force-static'

export function GET() {
  return new Response(buildLlmsTxt(getGuide(), getAllPosts(), getGuide('en'), getAllEnglishPosts()), {
    headers: { 'Content-Type': 'text/plain; charset=utf-8' },
  })
}
```

This article's Portuguese version shows an excerpt of the generated file,
cut off on purpose at the first block of links. Further down, the file has
a section with the trap, and reproducing it would open a new path to it.
For the same reason, neither version links to the file itself. Every human
visit from these pages would land in the fetch count my prediction above
is still measuring.

## How to check whether anyone reads your llms.txt

**1. Confirm the file responds.**

```bash
# illustrative: replace with your domain
curl -sI https://your-domain.com/llms.txt
```

You should see status `200` and a text `content-type`. If you get a 404
here, everything else is theory.

**2. Count the fetches in your access log.** Filter for `/llms.txt` and
compare the count with `/robots.txt` over the same period. That gives you
the order of magnitude without computing a share of total requests. Leave
out your own requests, too. In the pilot, my own network accounted for 24%
of all recorded traffic.

**3. Be careful how you name agents.** Your log shows the user agent each
request claims. Unless you verify identity (against the IP ranges the
vendor publishes, or with reverse DNS), say "requests identifying
themselves as X," not "X read my file." The
[case study](/en/case-studies/ai-crawler-detection) shows how I verified
identity in the pilot.

**4. To learn whether anyone follows the file, use a trap link**, a URL
that exists only in llms.txt. Learn from my mistake and keep its address
out of any versioned file in a public repository, out of the sitemap, and
off every page.

**5. Calculate how many fetches you need before you start.** With zero
hits, the 95% upper bound is `1 − 0.05^(1/n)`.

| Fetches of llms.txt (n) | Upper bound with 0 hits |
|---|---|
| 9 | 28% |
| 30 | 9.5% |
| 59 | 5% |
| 100 | 3% |

At the pilot's rate of 9 fetches in 57 days, reaching 59 fetches would take
a little over a year. A small site probably can't get a strong answer to
"does anyone follow my llms.txt?" within a few months. It's better to know
that before you start than to find out at closing, as I did.
