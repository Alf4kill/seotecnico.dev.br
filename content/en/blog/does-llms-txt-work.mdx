llms.txt is a proposed Markdown file, served at the site root (`/llms.txt`),
that summarises the site and lists the content language models should read.
On this site, between 25 July and 20 September 2026, the file was read 9
times, and no client (browser, crawler or agent) followed the link that
existed only inside it. That does not prove llms.txt is useless. It proves
that, here, almost nobody reads it.

For Google Search, the question already has an official answer: it ignores
the file. The rest of this article is about what a small site can measure on
its own, where my test failed and what I am measuring now.

## What llms.txt is

The proposal was published by Jeremy Howard on 3 September 2024, at
[llmstxt.org](https://llmstxt.org/). The format is deliberately simple: an H1
with the site's name, the only required part, a summary in a blockquote,
optional paragraphs, and H2 sections with lists of Markdown links. A section
named `Optional` marks what can be dropped when the model's context is short.

Two details of the proposal tend to vanish from summaries. First: the stated
target is inference, the moment a model answers someone, not training.
Second: the proposal also suggests serving a Markdown version of each page,
at the same address with `.md` appended or replacing the extension. That
second idea comes back later, in H14.

llms.txt is not a directive. robots.txt says what a crawler may crawl;
llms.txt only suggests what to read. Listing a URL in it does not grant
access, and leaving a URL out does not protect it.

### What the vendors document

Google has a written position. Its [official guide to optimising for
generative AI features in
Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide),
updated on 10 July 2026, says you do not need to create AI text files,
Markdown or new markup to appear in Search, because Google Search ignores
them. Keeping such a file for other services, the same guide says, neither
helps nor harms visibility in Search.

For OpenAI and Anthropic, on their official crawler pages, consulted in
October 2026, I found no mention of reading third-party sites' llms.txt.
OpenAI's documentation publishes its own llms.txt, which shows use of the
format as a publisher, not as a reader. Absence of documentation does not
prove they do not read it. It is only what is written.

## How I tested it: a link that exists only in llms.txt

Counting reads of the file is easy: filter requests to `/llms.txt`. The hard
part is knowing whether anyone uses what they read. For that, in the crawler
detection pilot I ran on this site ([the full case
study](/en/case-studies/ai-crawler-detection)), I put a link in llms.txt to a
URL that existed nowhere else on the site: no page linked to it and it was not
in the sitemap. I called that URL Trap B.

The logic is direct. If a client reads llms.txt and follows its links, at some
point it hits the trap. If the trap is never hit, nobody followed the file, at
least not through that link.

The pilot was planned for 90 days. I closed it at 57, from 25 July to 20
September 2026, at the deploy of the site's redesign. The early close and its
reason are stated in the pilot's write-up.

## The result: 9 reads, 0 clicks

| Measure (25 Jul to 20 Sep 2026) | Count |
|---|---|
| Reads of `/llms.txt` | 9 |
| Hits on Trap B (link only in llms.txt) | 0 |
| Reads of `/robots.txt`, same period | 1,191 |

The comparison with robots.txt is only an order of magnitude: in the same
period, the access-rules file was read 1,191 times, and llms.txt 9. That is
two orders of magnitude of difference in clients' interest in the two files.

Zero in nine looks conclusive, but it is not. With zero hits in n reads, the
one-sided 95% upper bound for the true follow rate is `1 − 0.05^(1/n)`. For
n = 9, that gives 28%. In plain terms: the result is consistent with up to
28% of reads following the link. The verdict I recorded for the hypothesis
was "sustained, but the bound is useless": with nine reads, "nothing follows
llms.txt" cannot be told apart from "almost nothing reads it". The finding is
the exposure, not the zero.

### Who read it

The pilot's write-up does not identify the readers, and I do not name anyone
here either. There is a partial read: in an interim 28-day pull, 16 August to
12 September 2026, excluding my own network's traffic, the file was read 5
times, all by clients that did **not** declare themselves AI agents, and none
by a declared AI agent.

That speaks to a separate prediction, registered the day the file went live:
**no read of `/llms.txt` by a major vendor's AI agent within 90 days**. The
window closes on 23 October 2026, and the verdict goes into this article when
it does. Until then, the 5 reads in that slice are an interim observation,
not a result.

## Why the test does not decide

There are two problems, and the second is worse than the first.

**Low power.** Nine reads is little. A simple calculation shows the size of
the problem: if 1 in 10 readers followed the link, the chance of still seeing
zero hits in 9 reads would be 0.9⁹, about 39%. The test had almost four
chances in ten of returning zero even with a real effect.

**Confounding by the public repository.** The trap's address was in a
versioned file of the site's repository, which is public on GitHub. Any hit
on the trap could have come from someone, or some bot, reading the repository
rather than llms.txt. So even if the trap had been hit, that would not have
proved anyone follows llms.txt. The design could only say something if it
returned zero, and zero, with 9 reads, says little.

The mistake was mine, and I tell it because it is the kind anyone repeats when
setting up such a test in an open-source project: the file you use to
configure the trap is also a discovery channel.

The lesson I recorded when closing the pilot was to pre-register power, not
just the hypothesis. Another hypothesis of the same pilot, H5, showed the
problem at closing: it needed roughly ten times more crawler visits than the
site received, and no extension of the window would fix that. Since then,
every hypothesis registered here states, next to its window, the smallest
effect it can detect.

## What came next: asking for Markdown with `Accept: text/markdown`

The proposal's second idea, serving Markdown instead of HTML, has a variant
that needs no new URLs: content negotiation. The client sends the `Accept:
text/markdown` header, and the server decides the response format. Cloudflare
documents exactly this mechanism in its [Markdown for
Agents](https://developers.cloudflare.com/fundamentals/reference/markdown-for-agents/)
feature, which converts HTML to Markdown at the edge when the client asks.

Before building anything, I want to know whether anyone asks. H14 runs from
25 September to 24 December 2026 and only measures: every response stays
HTML. The hypothesis is that at least one real request asks a page for
Markdown within the window. Going by the pilot's volume, the window should
carry around 2,700 requests that do not come from a browser, so a zero would
bound the Markdown-asking share at about 0.1% of them. The decision rule was
published before it started: serving Markdown will only be built if the
requests come from at least 5 distinct networks or from 2 agents with
verified identity.

To see what your own server does when a client asks for Markdown:

```bash
# illustrative: replace with your domain
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{content_type}\n" \
  -H "Accept: text/markdown" \
  https://your-domain.com/
```

If the answer is `text/html`, the server ignores the preference, which is the
default behaviour of most sites and this one's during H14.

In parallel, the second version of the detection instrument, with hypotheses
H7 to H13 pre-registered, plans discovery channels whose addresses never
enter a versioned file, plus a trap that exists only in the repository, to
measure the size of the leak. It is the direct fix for the Trap B confound.
It is registered, not yet built.

## Is it worth creating an llms.txt?

The honest answer is conditional.

- **If the file can be generated by code** from something the site already
  has, such as the post index, the cost of keeping it is close to zero.
  Creating it does no harm: Google itself says the file neither helps nor
  hurts in Search.
- **If it depends on manual maintenance**, it ages. An llms.txt written by hand
  and forgotten points to URLs that changed or vanished, and whoever follows
  it gets a wrong map of the site.
- **If the goal is to appear in AI answers**, the file is not where the effort
  belongs. When I tested five assistants between September and October 2026,
  the answers carried the text of the served HTML of the page they opened
  ([the experiment, with dates, plans and
  conditions](/en/blog/which-ai-assistants-run-javascript)). The test neither
  offered nor measured llms.txt; what it shows is that the HTML is enough to
  be read. To check what that HTML delivers in the `<head>`, the [meta tag
  validator](/ferramentas/validador-meta-tags) reads the same raw server
  response (its interface is in Portuguese).

This site keeps serving the file. It is generated by code and is part of the
measurement instrument, so keeping it costs little and still produces data.

## In Next.js: how this site generates the file

This site's llms.txt comes from `src/lib/llms-txt.ts`, from the same source
as the sitemap: the guide and the list of posts read from `/content`. The
file's header reuses `site.description`, the site's central description,
instead of separately written text. If the description changes, llms.txt
changes with it.

```ts
// src/lib/llms-txt.ts (lines 71–79)
export function buildLlmsTxt(guide: Post, posts: Post[], guideEn?: Post, postsEn: Post[] = []): string {
  const sections = [
    `# ${site.name}`,
    '',
    `> ${site.description} Conteúdo em português (pt-BR), escrito por ${site.author.name}. Cada técnica documentada aqui está implementada neste próprio domínio e medida com Google Search Console, CrUX e experimentos antes/depois — nada é publicado antes de ser implementado e medido.`,
    '',
    '## Guia (pilar)',
    '',
    linkLine('/guia/seo-tecnico-nextjs', guide.frontmatter.title, guide.frontmatter.description),
```

The file is written in Portuguese, the site's primary language. The
blockquote says the content is in Portuguese, written by the author, and that
every technique documented is implemented on this domain and measured before
it is published.

The file is served by a static route handler, `src/app/llms.txt/route.ts`,
built at deploy time like `feed.xml`: new content arrives by commit, which
already triggers the deploy.

```ts
// src/app/llms.txt/route.ts (lines 6–12)
export const dynamic = 'force-static'

export function GET() {
  return new Response(buildLlmsTxt(getGuide(), getAllPosts(), getGuide('en'), getAllEnglishPosts()), {
    headers: { 'Content-Type': 'text/plain; charset=utf-8' },
  })
}
```

The excerpt of the file is in the [Portuguese version of this
article](/blog/llms-txt-funciona). It stops at the first block of links on
purpose: further down, the file has the trap's section, and reproducing it
would open a new path to it. For the same reason, neither version links to the
file itself: every human visit from here would enter the read count the
prediction above is still measuring.

For the rest of this site's reference implementation, see the [technical SEO
guide for Next.js](/en/guide/technical-seo-nextjs).

## How to check whether anyone reads your llms.txt

**1. Confirm the file responds.**

```bash
# illustrative: replace with your domain
curl -sI https://your-domain.com/llms.txt
```

Expect a `200` status and a text `content-type`. A 404 here means everything
else is theory.

**2. Count the reads in the access log**, filtering the `/llms.txt` path, and
compare with `/robots.txt` over the same period. The comparison gives the
order of magnitude without needing a share of all requests. Remove your own
requests: in the pilot, my network's traffic was 24% of everything recorded.

**3. Name the agents carefully.** In your log, what exists is the user agent
the request declares. Without verifying identity (vendor-published IP ranges,
reverse DNS), the correct sentence is "requests that declare themselves as
X", not "X read my file". The [case
study](/en/case-studies/ai-crawler-detection) shows how I did that
verification.

**4. To know whether anyone follows the file, use a trap link**: a URL that
exists only in llms.txt. And learn from my mistake. The address cannot be in
a versioned file of a public repository, in the sitemap, or on any page.

**5. Before starting, work out how many reads you need.** With zero hits, the
95% upper bound is `1 − 0.05^(1/n)`:

| Reads of llms.txt (n) | Upper bound with 0 hits |
|---|---|
| 9 | 28% |
| 30 | 9.5% |
| 59 | 5% |
| 100 | 3% |

At the pilot's pace (9 reads in 57 days), reaching 59 reads would take a
little over a year. On a small site, the question "does anyone follow my
llms.txt?" probably has no strong answer within a few months. Better to know
that before starting than to find out at closing, as I did.
