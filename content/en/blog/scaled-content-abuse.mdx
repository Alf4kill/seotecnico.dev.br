Scaled content abuse is Google's spam policy against sites that produce many pages with the primary purpose of manipulating rankings, without helping readers. The policy applies to any method: AI, a script or a person filling in a template. The agency model, with a fixed block of served cities, one page per keyword variation and titles generated in series, shows up piece by piece in three passages of the documentation: the keyword stuffing example, the doorway definition and the scale policy itself.

There is no lab measurement here. The article takes the HTML a typical template serves, shows which sentence of the documentation describes each piece, and ends with a step-by-step audit of your own.

## What Google calls scaled content abuse

In the spam policies, scaled content abuse is generating many pages with the primary purpose of manipulating rankings rather than helping users. It usually comes as a large volume of unoriginal, low-value content, and the text closes on the central point: it does not matter how the content was created.

The listed examples, paraphrased:

- using generative AI or a similar tool to generate many pages without adding value;
- scraping feeds, search results or other content, including with synonym swapping or automatic translation;
- stitching together passages from different pages without adding anything;
- creating several sites to hide the scale;
- creating many pages that make little sense to a reader but contain the searched keywords.

The change that brings the agency template into scope dates from March 2024. The old "automatically generated content" policy became a policy about scale, and the announcement made explicit that it covers content produced by automation, by people or by both. A writer filling in the template city by city is covered the same way as a script.

AI is not the target. Google's guidance on generative AI content says it can help with research and with structuring original content; the problem is generating many pages without value. The criterion is scale without value, not the tool.

| When | What changed in the documentation |
|---|---|
| 2022 | Google launches the helpful content system, with a signal applied to the whole site |
| March 2024 | Automatically generated content becomes scaled content abuse; the helpful content system becomes part of the core ranking systems |
| May 2026 | The spam policies also apply to generative AI responses in Search |
| October 2026 | The generative AI content guidance starts pointing to the rater guidelines' sections on scale and low effort |

Google's text now names these practices. No official source marks a date after which the agency model stopped delivering results, and I am not going to invent one.

## What an agency template puts in the HTML

To make it concrete, I will use a fictional site: Example Drains, a service company with a classic agency-built site. It does not exist and has no metrics.

The template produces three things in the served HTML:

1. **A fixed block on every page**: "We serve the neighbourhoods X, Y, Z..." with the list of neighbourhoods and cities, followed by an institutional paragraph identical on every URL.
2. **One page per keyword or per city**: `/drain-cleaning-south-side`, `/drain-cleaning-24h`, `/drain-cleaning-in-springfield`, each with the same body and the keyword swapped.
3. **`<title>`, H1 and `alt` generated from the keyword**: the same mould with a different word on each page.

## The served-areas block: the literal keyword stuffing example

Keyword stuffing, in Google's definition, is loading a page with keywords or numbers to manipulate rankings, often in a list or group, unnaturally or out of context. One of the examples is "blocks of text that list cities and regions", and the full sentence adds the qualifier: the ones a web page is trying to rank for.

That qualifier is what matters. The problem is not saying where the company works; it is a block whose job is to put city names on the page in order to rank for them. The SEO Starter Guide makes the same point: repeating the same words over and over, even in variations, is tiring for users and goes against the spam policies.

The rater guidelines have a category called filler: low-effort material that inflates the page, worse when there is a lot of it or when it pushes the helpful content down. That a neighbourhood block repeated on every page fits there is my reading, not Google's text: the guidelines do not mention this case.

What the documentation does **not** say: how Google weighs a body block repeated across the whole site, or how many city names a page can carry. Any number on that comes from third parties.

## One page per keyword variation: doorway and scale

The doorway policy describes pages created to rank for specific, similar queries, which lead to intermediate pages less useful than the final destination. Two of its examples fit the template:

- pages targeting specific regions or cities that funnel visitors to a single page;
- substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than to a browsable hierarchy.

Google's guide to optimizing for generative AI features closes the other end: creating separate content for every variation of how people search, when done mainly to manipulate rankings or AI responses, violates the scaled content abuse policy. The guide adds that a high number of pages does not make a site more relevant, and that the systems understand synonyms without one page per phrasing.

**Not every page per city is a doorway.** The examples are about funnelling and substantially similar pages. A location page with information specific to the place (team, address, response time, local particulars) falls outside that description. The useful test: if I swap the city name, does the rest of the page stay true? If it does, the page has nothing of its own about the place.

There is a more sophisticated version: rewriting the text for each keyword. Those pages are not textual duplicates, so a duplicate-text checker flags nothing. But the policy does not talk about identical text; it talks about substantially similar pages and about scale. Saying the same thing in other words still fits that description.

The rater guidelines give examples of this kind in their sections on scale and low effort: error-code pages filled in from a template, product names swapped into a generic text, hundreds of articles in the same mould, repetitive questions and answers. A caveat: those guidelines instruct raters who evaluate the quality of the systems, and Google states that their ratings do not directly influence ranking.

## Title, H1 and alt generated in series

The title link guide is direct: "Avoid repeated or boilerplate text in `<title>` elements". The guide's bad example is a long title shared by every page that changes a single piece of information. The suggested solution is to generate the title from what the page actually contains.

When it detects this, Google may build another title for the result from headings, page text or anchor text. If that happens to the template's pages, the search result stops showing the title the mould generated.

The generative AI content guidance extends the review to metadata: `<title>`, meta description, structured data and image `alt` text. If the template generates those fields from the keyword, they inherit the body's problem.

**A title identical to the H1 is not a documented problem.** The title link guide points the other way: make the main title clear, for example by putting the title text in the first visible `<h1>`. On this site, each article's H1 is rendered from the title in the frontmatter. What the guide criticizes is the same title across pages, not a title equal to the H1 of the same page.

## How Google reads a set of near-identical pages

The canonicalization documentation describes the mechanism: when indexing, Google identifies the primary content of each page; when several have very similar primary content, it groups them, picks the most complete one as canonical and crawls the others less often.

Duplicate content on its own does not violate the spam policies. The canonicalization page says so, and the SEO Starter Guide adds that it is inefficient but will not cause a manual action. Consolidating those URLs in practice is covered in [redirects and canonicals in Next.js](/blog/redirects-canonicals-nextjs) (in Portuguese).

What can be stated is conditional. **If** the template's pages differ only by keyword or city, the part that varies is small next to the shared part, and the documented behaviour for very similar primary content is to cluster the pages and index one. Google has never written this about city pages; it is the general mechanism applied to the case.

In words: Google identifies the primary content of each template URL. Since it is nearly the same on all of them, they fall into the same cluster. One becomes the indexed canonical, and the others are crawled less often.

On site versus page: between 2022 and 2024, the helpful content system applied a signal to the whole site, now documented as historical. Today, the ranking systems guide says they work at the page level but also use site-wide signals, without saying which. The sentence saying Google presumes individual pages match the quality of the rest of the domain does exist, but it sits inside the site reputation abuse policy, which deals with third-party content. It is an indication, not a rule about templates.

## What is not a problem (and circulates as if it were)

Many content "rules" that circulate in SEO have no basis in the documentation:

| What circulates | What Google says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A page needs a minimum word count | Length alone does not matter; there is no ideal word count | SEO Starter Guide; generative AI guide |
| Breaking text into small chunks helps AI | Not required | Generative AI guide |
| Headings need the right order and number | For Search, order does not matter and there is no ideal number | SEO Starter Guide |
| A title identical to the H1 is a mistake | Not documented; the guide suggests the title text in the first visible h1 | Title link guide |
| You must cover every long-tail variation | Unnecessary; the systems understand synonyms | Generative AI guide |
| Text that "looks like AI" is punished | Using AI is not the criterion; value is | Generative AI content guidance; rater guidelines |
| Duplicate content gets punished | It does not violate the spam policies or cause a manual action | Canonicalization; SEO Starter Guide |

The policy problem lies in scale without value, keyword blocks and doorway pages. Formatting does not enter that account.

## In Next.js: `generateStaticParams` over a keyword list is a doorway generator

In the [technical SEO guide for Next.js](/en/guide/technical-seo-nextjs) I use the App Router as the reference implementation. And the agency template fits in a few lines of it.

Illustrative: it does not exist in this repository.

```tsx
// app/services/[slug]/page.tsx
const variations = ['drain-cleaning-south-side', 'drain-cleaning-24h', 'drain-cleaning-in-springfield']

export function generateStaticParams() {
  return variations.map((slug) => ({ slug }))
}

type Props = { params: Promise<{ slug: string }> }

export async function generateMetadata({ params }: Props) {
  const { slug } = await params
  return { title: `${slug.replaceAll('-', ' ')} | Brand` }
}

export default async function Page({ params }: Props) {
  const { slug } = await params
  return <ServicePage keyword={slug.replaceAll('-', ' ')} />
}
```

The build emits one page per item in the list, with the same body and a title in the "keyword | Brand" format. Googlebot evaluates the HTML that arrives, not the code that generated it.

The real code of this site's English article route:

```tsx
// src/app/(en)/en/blog/[slug]/page.tsx
export const dynamicParams = false

export function generateStaticParams() {
  return getAllEnglishPosts().map(({ frontmatter }) => ({ slug: frontmatter.slug }))
}
```

```tsx
// src/app/(en)/en/blog/[slug]/page.tsx
export async function generateMetadata({ params }: PageProps): Promise<Metadata> {
  const { slug } = await params
  const post = getEnglishPostBySlug(slug)
  if (!post) return {}

  const { frontmatter } = post
  const path = `/en/blog/${frontmatter.slug}`
  return buildMetadata({
    title: frontmatter.title,
    absoluteTitle: true,
    description: frontmatter.description,
    path,
    lang: 'en',
    article: {
      publishedTime: frontmatter.datePublished,
      modifiedTime: frontmatter.dateModified,
    },
    ogImage: { path: `${path}/opengraph-image`, alt: frontmatter.title },
  })
}
```

The shape is similar; the origin of the list is not. Here, each route exists because an MDX file was written, and any other slug returns 404 because of `dynamicParams = false`. The title comes from each article's frontmatter, not from a "keyword | Brand" mould.

The difference is not in `generateStaticParams` but in what the list represents: written content or keywords. This site has no pages per city or per variation, so I have no data of my own on how Google would treat such a template.

## How to check whether your template falls into this

Five steps, from the most objective to the most subjective:

1. **URL Inspection in Search Console**, on two or three template pages. Compare the "User-declared canonical" with the "Google-selected canonical". If Google picks another template page, it is treating the two as duplicates. Use the indexed data: according to Search Console Help, the live test does not check duplicate status or predict the canonical.
2. **Page indexing report.** Look for "Duplicate without user-selected canonical" and "Duplicate, Google chose different canonical than user". Many template URLs under those reasons mean Google clustered the pages and indexed one. It is not an error in itself; it is canonicalization at work on near-identical pages.
3. **Manual actions report.** Confirm it is empty. There is no action named after the policy: scaled content abuse appears under "Major spam problems", doorways under "Thin content with little or no added value", and keyword stuffing under "Hidden text and/or keyword stuffing". An empty report does not mean the automated systems agree: Google detects violations through automated systems and, as needed, human review, and it is human review that can result in a manual action.
4. **Crawl the titles.** Export the titles with Screaming Frog or similar, strip the variable part (city, neighbourhood, keyword) and group the rest. Dozens of URLs with the same title is the boilerplate the guide describes. To check page by page, the [meta tag validator](/ferramentas/validador-meta-tags) shows title, description and H1 side by side (its interface is in Portuguese).
5. **The rater test.** Open several template pages in a row. The guidelines tell raters to judge scale after looking at several pages, not one. It is a heuristic, not a measurement, but it usually makes the pattern obvious.

## If the site is already like this: what Google documents about recovery

The core updates page says improvements can take months to be recognized, sometimes only at the next core update. Since December 2025, it also mentions smaller, unannounced core updates as another path. And there is no guarantee of impact.

Removing content is treated as a last resort. The same page says that if you are considering deleting whole sections, they were probably created for search engines, and removing them can help the rest of the site. Nothing there guarantees traffic recovery from removing the city block, and I do not guarantee it either.

If I had to order the work, with the caveat that it depends on the site:

- **Consolidate** the variation pages into one page per real service, redirecting the old URLs. In Next.js, `permanent: true` returns 308, not 301; details in [redirects and canonicals in Next.js](/blog/redirects-canonicals-nextjs) (in Portuguese).
- **Replace the city block** with real coverage information where it helps the reader, such as response time by region.
- **Rewrite the titles** from distinct fields of each page, not from a mould with the keyword.
