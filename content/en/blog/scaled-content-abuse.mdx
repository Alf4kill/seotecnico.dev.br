Scaled content abuse is Google's spam policy against sites that churn out many pages mainly to manipulate rankings rather than help readers. It applies no matter how the pages are made: AI, a script or a person filling in a template. The typical agency template, with a fixed list of cities served, one page per keyword variation and titles stamped out from a pattern, maps piece by piece onto three passages in Google's documentation: the keyword stuffing example, the doorway definition and the scaled content policy itself.

There's no lab measurement in this one. The article takes the HTML a typical template serves, shows which sentence of the documentation describes each piece, and ends with a step-by-step audit you can run on your own site.

## What Google calls scaled content abuse

In Google's spam policies, scaled content abuse means generating many pages with the primary purpose of manipulating rankings rather than helping users. It typically takes the form of a large volume of unoriginal, low-value content, and the policy ends on the key point: it doesn't matter how the content was created.

The examples it lists, paraphrased:

- using generative AI or similar tools to generate many pages without adding value;
- scraping feeds, search results or other content, including with synonym swapping or automated translation;
- stitching together passages from different pages without adding anything;
- spreading content across several sites to hide the scale;
- creating many pages that make little sense to a reader but contain the keywords people search for.

The change that brought agency templates into scope came in March 2024. The old "automatically generated content" policy became a policy about scale, and the announcement made it explicit that it covers content produced by automation, by people or by both. A writer filling in a template city by city is covered just as much as a script.

AI isn't the target. Google's guidance on generative AI content says it can help with research and with structuring original content; the problem is generating many pages without value. What counts is scale without value, not the tool.

| When | What changed in the documentation |
|---|---|
| 2022 | Google launches the helpful content system, with a signal applied site-wide |
| March 2024 | "Automatically generated content" becomes scaled content abuse; the helpful content system is folded into the core ranking systems |
| May 2026 | The spam policies are extended to generative AI responses in Search |
| October 2026 | The generative AI content guidance starts pointing to the rater guidelines' sections on scale and low effort |

Google's documentation now names these practices explicitly. No official source gives a date after which the agency model stopped working, and I'm not going to make one up.

## What an agency template puts in the HTML

To make this concrete, I'll use a fictional site: Example Drains, a service company with a classic agency-built website. It doesn't exist and has no metrics.

The template puts three things into the served HTML:

1. **A fixed block on every page**: "We serve X, Y, Z..." with a list of neighborhoods and cities, followed by a boilerplate company paragraph that's identical on every URL.
2. **One page per keyword or per city**: `/drain-cleaning-south-side`, `/drain-cleaning-24h`, `/drain-cleaning-in-springfield`, each with the same body and a different keyword swapped in.
3. **`<title>`, H1 and `alt` generated from the keyword**: the same pattern with a different word on each page.

## The service-area block: a textbook keyword stuffing example

Google defines keyword stuffing as loading a page with keywords or numbers to manipulate rankings, often in a list or group, unnaturally or out of context. One of its examples is "blocks of text that list cities and regions", and the full sentence adds a qualifier: the ones a web page is trying to rank for.

That qualifier is what matters. Saying where the company operates isn't the problem; a block whose job is to put city names on the page so it ranks for them is. The SEO Starter Guide makes the same point: repeating the same words over and over, even in variations, is tiresome for users and goes against the spam policies.

The rater guidelines have a category called filler: low-effort material that pads out a page, which is worse when there's a lot of it or when it pushes the helpful content down. That a neighborhood list repeated on every page belongs in this category is my reading, not Google's wording; the guidelines don't mention this case.

What the documentation does **not** say: how Google weighs a block of body text repeated across the whole site, or how many city names a page can carry. Any number you see on that comes from third parties.

## One page per keyword variation: doorway and scale

The doorway policy describes pages created to rank for specific, similar queries that lead users to intermediate pages less useful than the final destination. Two of its examples fit the template:

- pages targeting specific regions or cities that funnel visitors to a single page;
- substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than to a browsable hierarchy.

Google's guide to optimizing for generative AI features covers the other side: creating separate content for every variation of how people search, when done mainly to manipulate rankings or AI responses, violates the scaled content abuse policy. The guide adds that having lots of pages doesn't make a site more relevant, and that its systems understand synonyms without needing one page per phrasing.

**Not every city page is a doorway.** The examples are about funneling and substantially similar pages. A location page with information specific to that place (the team, the address, response times, local specifics) falls outside that description. A useful test: if you swap out the city name, does the rest of the page still hold true? If it does, the page has nothing of its own to say about the place.

There's a more sophisticated version: rewriting the text for each keyword. Those pages aren't textual duplicates, so a duplicate-content checker won't flag anything. But the policy isn't about identical text; it's about substantially similar pages and about scale. Saying the same thing in different words still fits that description.

The rater guidelines give examples like this in their sections on scale and low effort: error-code pages filled in from a template, product names swapped into generic text, hundreds of articles cut from the same pattern, repetitive Q&As. One caveat: those guidelines are instructions for raters who evaluate how well Google's systems work, and Google says their ratings don't directly affect rankings.

## Titles, H1s and alt text stamped out from a pattern

The title link guide is blunt: "Avoid repeated or boilerplate text in `<title>` elements". Its bad example is a long title shared by every page, with only one piece of information changing. The suggested fix is to generate the title from what the page actually contains.

When Google detects this, it may build a different title for the search result from headings, page text or anchor text. If that happens to the template's pages, search results stop showing the title the pattern generated.

The generative AI content guidance extends the review to metadata: `<title>`, meta description, structured data and image `alt` text. If the template generates those fields from the keyword, they inherit the same problem as the body text.

**A title identical to the H1 isn't a documented problem.** The title link guide actually suggests the opposite: make the main title clear, for example by using the title text in the first visible `<h1>`. On this site, each article's H1 is rendered from the title in its frontmatter. What the guide criticizes is the same title across many pages, not a title that matches the H1 on the same page.

## How Google reads a set of near-identical pages

The canonicalization documentation describes the mechanism: during indexing, Google identifies each page's primary content; when several pages have very similar primary content, it groups them, picks the most complete one as the canonical and crawls the others less often.

Duplicate content on its own doesn't violate the spam policies. The canonicalization page says so, and the SEO Starter Guide adds that it's inefficient but won't trigger a manual action. How to consolidate those URLs in practice is covered in [redirects and canonicals in Next.js](/en/blog/nextjs-redirects-canonicals).

What can be said is conditional. **If** the template's pages differ only by keyword or city, the part that varies is small compared with the shared part, and the documented behavior for very similar primary content is to cluster the pages and index one of them. Google has never written this about city pages specifically; it's the general mechanism applied to this case.

Put simply: Google identifies the primary content of each template URL. Since it's nearly the same on all of them, they land in the same cluster. One becomes the indexed canonical, and the rest get crawled less often.

On the question of site versus page: between 2022 and 2024, the helpful content system applied a site-wide signal, which is now documented as historical. Today, the ranking systems guide says the systems work at the page level but also use site-wide signals, without saying which ones. The sentence about Google presuming individual pages match the quality of the rest of the domain does exist, but it's part of the site reputation abuse policy, which deals with third-party content. It's a hint, not a rule about templates.

## What isn't a problem (but gets passed around as if it were)

Plenty of content "rules" that get passed around in SEO have no basis in the documentation:

| The claim | What Google says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A page needs a minimum word count | Length alone doesn't matter; there's no ideal word count | SEO Starter Guide; generative AI guide |
| Breaking text into small chunks helps AI | Not required | Generative AI guide |
| Headings need the right order and number | For Search, order doesn't matter and there's no ideal number | SEO Starter Guide |
| A title identical to the H1 is a mistake | Not documented; the guide suggests using the title text in the first visible h1 | Title link guide |
| You have to cover every long-tail variation | Unnecessary; the systems understand synonyms | Generative AI guide |
| Text that "sounds like AI" gets penalized | Using AI isn't the criterion; value is | Generative AI content guidance; rater guidelines |
| Duplicate content gets penalized | It doesn't violate the spam policies or trigger a manual action | Canonicalization; SEO Starter Guide |

The policy is concerned with scale without value, keyword blocks and doorway pages. Formatting doesn't come into it.

## In Next.js: `generateStaticParams` over a keyword list is a doorway generator

In the [technical SEO guide for Next.js](/en/guide/technical-seo-nextjs), I use the App Router as the reference implementation, and the agency template takes just a few lines of it.

This is illustrative; it doesn't exist in this repository.

```tsx
// app/services/[slug]/page.tsx
const variations = ['drain-cleaning-south-side', 'drain-cleaning-24h', 'drain-cleaning-in-springfield']

export function generateStaticParams() {
  return variations.map((slug) => ({ slug }))
}

type Props = { params: Promise<{ slug: string }> }

export async function generateMetadata({ params }: Props) {
  const { slug } = await params
  return { title: `${slug.replaceAll('-', ' ')} | Brand` }
}

export default async function Page({ params }: Props) {
  const { slug } = await params
  return <ServicePage keyword={slug.replaceAll('-', ' ')} />
}
```

The build emits one page per item in the list, each with the same body and a title in the "keyword | Brand" format. Googlebot judges the HTML it receives, not the code that generated it.

Here's the actual code for this site's English article route:

```tsx
// src/app/(en)/en/blog/[slug]/page.tsx
export const dynamicParams = false

export function generateStaticParams() {
  return getAllEnglishPosts().map(({ frontmatter }) => ({ slug: frontmatter.slug }))
}
```

```tsx
// src/app/(en)/en/blog/[slug]/page.tsx
export async function generateMetadata({ params }: PageProps): Promise<Metadata> {
  const { slug } = await params
  const post = getEnglishPostBySlug(slug)
  if (!post) return {}

  const { frontmatter } = post
  const path = `/en/blog/${frontmatter.slug}`
  return buildMetadata({
    title: frontmatter.title,
    absoluteTitle: true,
    description: frontmatter.description,
    path,
    lang: 'en',
    article: {
      publishedTime: frontmatter.datePublished,
      modifiedTime: frontmatter.dateModified,
    },
    ogImage: { path: `${path}/opengraph-image`, alt: frontmatter.title },
  })
}
```

The shape is similar; where the list comes from is not. Here, each route exists because someone wrote an MDX file, and any other slug returns a 404 thanks to `dynamicParams = false`. The title comes from each article's frontmatter, not from a "keyword | Brand" pattern.

The difference isn't `generateStaticParams`; it's what the list represents: written content, or keywords. This site has no city pages or keyword-variation pages, so I have no data of my own on how Google would treat a template like that.

## How to check whether your template falls into this

Five steps, from most objective to most subjective:

1. **URL Inspection in Search Console**, on two or three template pages. Compare the "User-declared canonical" with the "Google-selected canonical". If Google picks a different template page, it's treating the two as duplicates. Use the indexed data: according to Search Console Help, the live test doesn't check duplicate status or predict the canonical.
2. **Page indexing report.** Look for "Duplicate without user-selected canonical" and "Duplicate, Google chose different canonical than user". Lots of template URLs under those reasons means Google clustered the pages and indexed one. That isn't an error in itself; it's canonicalization doing its job on near-identical pages.
3. **Manual actions report.** Make sure it's empty. No action is named after the policy itself: scaled content abuse shows up under "Major spam problems", doorways under "Thin content with little or no added value", and keyword stuffing under "Hidden text and/or keyword stuffing". An empty report doesn't mean the automated systems agree with you: Google detects violations through automated systems and, when needed, human review, and it's human review that can lead to a manual action.
4. **Crawl the titles.** Export the titles with Screaming Frog or a similar tool, strip out the variable part (city, neighborhood, keyword) and group what's left. Dozens of URLs with the same title is exactly the boilerplate the guide describes. To check one page at a time, the [meta tag validator](/en/tools/meta-tag-validator) shows the title, description and H1 side by side.
5. **The rater test.** Open several template pages one after another. The guidelines tell raters to judge scale after looking at several pages, not just one. It's a heuristic, not a measurement, but it usually makes the pattern obvious.

## If your site already looks like this: what Google says about recovery

The core updates page says improvements can take months to be recognized, sometimes not until the next core update. Since December 2025, it also mentions smaller, unannounced core updates as another way changes get picked up. And there's no guarantee of any effect.

Removing content is treated as a last resort. The same page says that if you're thinking about deleting whole sections, they were probably created for search engines, and removing them may help the rest of the site. Nothing there promises that removing the city block will bring traffic back, and I don't promise that either.

If I had to prioritize the work, with the caveat that it depends on the site:

- **Consolidate** the variation pages into one page per actual service, redirecting the old URLs. In Next.js, `permanent: true` returns a 308, not a 301; details in [redirects and canonicals in Next.js](/en/blog/nextjs-redirects-canonicals).
- **Replace the city block** with real coverage information where it helps the reader, like response times by region.
- **Rewrite the titles** from fields that are genuinely different on each page, not from a pattern with the keyword dropped in.
