Scaled content abuse is a Google spam policy against sites that produce many
pages mainly to manipulate rankings, without helping the reader. It applies
however the pages get made, whether by AI, by a script, or by a person filling
in a template. A classic agency site comes with a fixed block of the cities
it serves, one page per keyword variation, and titles generated in series.
Google's documentation describes it piece by piece in three passages: the
keyword stuffing example, the doorway definition, and the scaled content
policy itself.

I didn't run a lab measurement for this article. Instead, I take the HTML a
typical template serves, show which sentence of the documentation describes
each piece, and end with a step-by-step audit you can run on your own site.

## What Google calls scaled content abuse

Google's spam policies define scaled content abuse as generating many pages
with the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings rather than helping
users. It typically means a large volume of unoriginal content that adds
little value. It closes with the point that matters most here, which is that
how the content was created doesn't matter.

Paraphrased, the examples it lists are:

- using generative AI or similar tools to generate many pages without adding
  value
- scraping feeds, search results, or other content, including with synonym
  swapping or automated translation
- stitching together passages from different pages without adding anything
- spreading content across several sites to hide the scale
- creating many pages that make little sense to a reader but contain the
  keywords people search for

A change in March 2024 is what pulls the agency template into scope. Google
turned its old "automatically generated content" policy into a policy about
scale, and the announcement spelled out that it covers content produced by
automation, by people, or by both. A writer filling in the template city by
city is covered just as a script is.

AI itself isn't the target. Google's guidance on AI-generated content says AI
can help with research and with structuring an original text. Generating many
pages without adding value is the problem. What counts is scale without value,
not the tool.

| When | What changed in the documentation |
|---|---|
| 2022 | Google launches the helpful content system, with a site-wide signal |
| March 2024 | "Automatically generated content" becomes scaled content abuse, and the helpful content system becomes part of the core ranking systems |
| May 2026 | Spam policies now also apply to generative AI responses in Search |
| October 2026 | The AI content guidance starts pointing to the rater guidelines' sections on scale and low effort |

Google's text now names these practices. No official source gives a date
after which the agency model stopped working, and I won't invent one.

## What an agency template puts in the HTML

To keep this concrete, picture Example Drains, a fictional service company
with a classic agency-built site. It doesn't exist and has no metrics. Its
template produces three things in the served HTML:

1. **A fixed block on every page.** "We serve X, Y, Z..." followed by the list
   of neighborhoods and cities, then a company paragraph that's identical on
   every URL.
2. **One page per keyword or per city.** `/drain-cleaning-south-side`,
   `/drain-cleaning-24h`, `/drain-cleaning-in-springfield`, each with the same
   body and a different keyword.
3. **`<title>`, H1, and `alt` generated from the keyword.** Every page uses the
   same pattern, with a different word dropped in.

## The service-area block: a textbook keyword stuffing example

Google defines keyword stuffing as filling a page with keywords or numbers to
manipulate rankings, often in lists, unnaturally or out of context. Among its
examples are "blocks of text that list cities and regions", which the full
sentence limits to cities and regions the web page is trying to rank for.

That qualifier is what matters. Saying where a company operates isn't the
problem. A block whose job is to put city names on the page so the page ranks
for them is.

Google's SEO Starter Guide adds that repeating the same words, even in
variations, is tiresome for users and goes against the spam policies.

Google's rater guidelines have a category called "filler," which covers
low-effort material that pads a page. They treat it as worse when there's a
lot of it or when it pushes the helpful content down. The guidelines don't
mention neighborhood blocks, so treating one that repeats on every page as
filler is my reading, not Google's text.

What the documentation does **not** say is how Google weighs a body block
repeated across a whole site, or how many city names a page can have. Any
number you see on that comes from third parties.

## One page per keyword variation: doorway and scale

Google's doorway policy covers pages created to rank for specific, similar
searches that lead users to intermediate pages less useful than the final
destination. Two of its examples fit the template:

- pages targeting specific regions or cities that funnel visitors to a single
  page
- substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than to a
  clearly defined, browsable hierarchy

Google's guide to optimizing for generative AI features comes at it from the
other side. It says that creating separate content for every variation of how
people search violates the scaled content abuse policy when you do it mainly
to manipulate rankings or AI responses. It also says that more pages don't
make a site more relevant, and that Google's systems understand synonyms
without one page per phrasing.

**Not every city page is a doorway.** Google's examples are about funneling
and about substantially similar pages. A location page with information
specific to the place, such as the local team, the address, response times, or
details about the region, falls outside that description. Here's a useful
test. Swap the city name and ask whether the rest of the page is still true.
If it is, the page has nothing specific to that place.

A more sophisticated variant rewrites the text for each keyword. Those pages
aren't textual duplicates, so a duplicate-text checker won't flag anything.
But the policy doesn't talk about identical text. It talks about substantially
similar pages and about scale, and saying the same thing in other words still
falls within that description.

You'll find examples of this kind in the rater guidelines' sections on scale
and low effort: error-code pages filled in from a template, product names
swapped into generic text, hundreds of articles cut from the same mold, and
repetitive Q&A. Keep in mind that those guidelines are for raters who evaluate
the quality of Google's systems, and Google says their ratings don't directly
influence rankings.

## Titles, H1s, and alt text stamped out from a pattern

Google's title link guide says it plainly:
"Avoid repeated or boilerplate text in `<title>` elements". Its bad example is
a long title shared by every page, with only one piece of information
changing. Instead, it suggests generating each title from what the page
actually contains.

When Google detects this problem, it may build a different title for the
search result from headings, page text, or anchor text. If that happens to
your template's pages, search results stop showing the title your template
generated.

Google's AI content guidance extends the review to metadata: `<title>`, meta
description, structured data, and image `alt` text. If your template generates
those fields from the keyword, they inherit the body's problem.

**A title identical to the H1 isn't a documented problem.** The title link
guide points the other way. It suggests making the main title clear, for
example by putting the title text in the first visible `<h1>`. On this site,
each article's H1 is rendered from its frontmatter title. The guide criticizes
the same title repeated across many pages, not a title that matches the H1 of
its own page.

## How Google reads a set of near-identical pages

Google's canonicalization docs describe the mechanism. During indexing, Google
identifies each page's primary content. When several pages have very similar
primary content, it groups them, picks the most complete one as canonical, and
crawls the others less often.

Duplicate content alone doesn't violate the spam policies. The
canonicalization page says so, and the SEO Starter Guide adds that it's
inefficient but doesn't cause a manual action. I cover how to consolidate
those URLs in
[redirects and canonicals in Next.js](/en/blog/nextjs-redirects-canonicals).

Only a conditional claim holds here. **If** your template pages differ only
by keyword or city, the part that varies is small next to the part they share.
When primary content is very similar, the documented behavior is to group the
pages and index one. Google never wrote this about city pages. It's the
general mechanism applied to this case.

Here are the same steps in text. Google identifies each template URL's
primary content. Because it's almost the same on all of them, they land in
the same cluster. One becomes the indexed canonical, and Google crawls the
others less often.

Site-level signals are a separate question. Between 2022 and 2024, the helpful
content system applied a site-wide signal, which Google now documents as
historical. Today, Google's ranking systems guide says its systems work at the
page level but also use site-wide signals, without saying which. Google does
write that it presumes individual pages match the quality of a domain's other
pages, but that sentence sits inside the site reputation abuse policy, which
deals with third-party content. It's a hint, not a rule about templates.

## What isn't a problem (but gets passed around as if it were)

Many content "rules" that circulate in Portuguese-language SEO writing in
Brazil have no basis in Google's documentation.

| Claim | What Google says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A page needs a minimum word count | Length alone does not matter, and there is no ideal word count | SEO Starter Guide, generative AI guide |
| Breaking text into small chunks helps AI | Not necessary | Generative AI guide |
| Headings need the right order and number | For Search, order does not matter and there is no ideal number | SEO Starter Guide |
| A title identical to the H1 is a mistake | Not documented. The guide suggests putting the title in the first visible h1 | Title link guide |
| You must cover every long-tail variation | Unnecessary. Systems understand synonyms | Generative AI guide |
| Text that "sounds like AI" is penalized | AI use is not the criterion. Value is | AI content guidance, rater guidelines |
| Duplicate content gets penalized | It does not violate the spam policies or cause a manual action | Canonicalization, SEO Starter Guide |

Where the policy does bite is scale without value, keyword blocks, and doorway
pages. Formatting has nothing to do with it.

## In Next.js: `generateStaticParams` over a keyword list is a doorway generator

In my [technical SEO guide for Next.js](/en/guide/technical-seo-nextjs), I use
the App Router as the reference implementation, and the agency template takes
a few lines of App Router code. This example is illustrative and doesn't exist
in this repository.

```tsx
// app/services/[slug]/page.tsx
const variations = ['drain-cleaning-south-side', 'drain-cleaning-24h', 'drain-cleaning-in-springfield']

export function generateStaticParams() {
  return variations.map((slug) => ({ slug }))
}

type Props = { params: Promise<{ slug: string }> }

export async function generateMetadata({ params }: Props) {
  const { slug } = await params
  return { title: `${slug.replaceAll('-', ' ')} | Brand` }
}

export default async function Page({ params }: Props) {
  const { slug } = await params
  return <ServicePage keyword={slug.replaceAll('-', ' ')} />
}
```

At build time, this emits one page per item in the list, each with the same
body and a title in the "keyword | Brand" format. Googlebot evaluates the HTML
that arrives, not the code that generated it.

Here's this site's real code for the English article route:

```tsx
// src/app/(en)/en/blog/[slug]/page.tsx
export const dynamicParams = false

export function generateStaticParams() {
  return getAllEnglishPosts().map(({ frontmatter }) => ({ slug: frontmatter.slug }))
}
```

```tsx
// src/app/(en)/en/blog/[slug]/page.tsx
export async function generateMetadata({ params }: PageProps): Promise<Metadata> {
  const { slug } = await params
  const post = getEnglishPostBySlug(slug)
  if (!post) return {}

  const { frontmatter } = post
  const path = `/en/blog/${frontmatter.slug}`
  return buildMetadata({
    title: frontmatter.title,
    absoluteTitle: true,
    description: frontmatter.description,
    path,
    lang: 'en',
    article: {
      publishedTime: frontmatter.datePublished,
      modifiedTime: frontmatter.dateModified,
    },
    ogImage: { path: `${path}/opengraph-image`, alt: frontmatter.title },
  })
}
```

It looks similar, but the list comes from somewhere else. Each route here
exists because an MDX file was written for it, and any other slug returns a
404 because of `dynamicParams = false`. Each article's title comes from its
frontmatter (details in
[how to generate title and description with the Metadata
API](/en/blog/nextjs-metadata-api)),
not from a "keyword | Brand" mold.

The difference isn't in `generateStaticParams`. It's in whether the list holds
written content or keywords. This site has no city pages or per-variation
pages, so I have no data of my own on how Google would treat a template like
that.

## How to check whether your template falls into this

These five checks run from the most objective to the most subjective:

1. **URL Inspection in Search Console.** Run it on two or three template pages
   and compare "User-declared canonical" with "Google-selected canonical". If
   Google picks a different template page, it's treating the two as
   duplicates. Read the indexed version's data. According to Search Console
   Help, the live test doesn't evaluate duplicate or canonical status.
2. **Page indexing report.** Look for
   "Duplicate without user-selected canonical" and
   "Duplicate, Google chose different canonical than user". If many template
   URLs show those statuses, Google grouped the pages and indexed one. That
   isn't an error in itself. It's canonicalization working on near-identical
   pages.
3. **Manual actions report.** Confirm it's empty. No manual action carries the
   policy's name. Scaled content abuse appears under "Major spam problems",
   doorways under "Thin content with little or no added value", and keyword
   stuffing under "Hidden text and/or keyword stuffing". An empty report
   doesn't mean the automated systems agree. Google detects violations through
   automated systems and, when necessary, human review. It's human review that
   can result in a manual action.
4. **Crawl the titles.** Export titles with Screaming Frog or a similar
   crawler, strip the variable part (city, neighborhood, keyword), and group
   what's left. Dozens of URLs with the same title are the boilerplate the
   guide describes. To check one page at a time, use the
   [meta tag validator](/en/tools/meta-tag-validator), which shows the title,
   description, and H1 side by side.
5. **The rater test.** Open several template pages in a row. The guidelines
   tell raters to judge scale after looking at several pages, not one. It's a
   heuristic, not a measurement, but it usually makes the pattern obvious.

## If your site already looks like this: what Google says about recovery

Google's core updates page says improvements can take months to be
recognized, sometimes not until the next core update. Since December 2025, it
also names smaller, unannounced core updates as another route. None of this
comes with a guarantee of impact.

Google treats removing content as a last resort. That page also says that if
you're thinking of deleting whole sections, they were probably made for search
engines, and removing them may help the rest of the site. Nothing there
guarantees that removing the city block will bring traffic back, and I don't
guarantee it either.

If I had to order the work, it would go like this, though it depends on the
site:

- **Consolidate** the per-variation pages into one page per real service, and
  redirect the old URLs. In Next.js, `permanent: true` produces a 308, not a
  301. I go through the details in
  [redirects and canonicals in Next.js](/en/blog/nextjs-redirects-canonicals).
- **Replace the city block** with real coverage information where it helps the
  reader, such as response times by region.
- **Rewrite the titles** from distinct fields of each page, not from a mold
  with the keyword in it.
