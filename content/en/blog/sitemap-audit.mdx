A sitemap audit compares three lists: what the sitemap promises, what each
listed URL answers, and what the site itself links to. Google asks for a
sitemap that carries the canonical URLs you want to see in search results.
That means any listed URL that answers with a redirect, a 404, or `noindex`, or
that points its canonical at another URL, contradicts the file. So does a page
the site links to everywhere but the sitemap leaves out.

On November 11, 2026, I measured four public sites and this one with the same
script, and I found a real case for every item in the checklist below. None of
them is a penalty. They're all signals that contradict each other, and you can
find every one of them from the outside, without access to the site's Search
Console.

## What a sitemap should list

Google's "Build and submit a sitemap" page, last updated July 8, 2026, says
three things that every item below is measured against.

- List the URLs you want in search results, in their canonical version. If
  the same content answers at several addresses, pick one and list only that
  one.
- Use absolute URLs. Google tries to crawl each one exactly as written.
- Treat the sitemap as a hint. Submitting it doesn't guarantee that Google
  downloads it, or that it crawls or indexes what's in it.

Google's page on canonical URLs, last updated July 10, 2026, adds a fourth.
Being in a sitemap is a weak canonicalization signal, weaker than a redirect
or `rel="canonical"`. Google asks you not to name one URL in the sitemap and
a different one in that page's canonical.

Google's documentation doesn't list things you should never put in a sitemap.
Each item in the checklist is wrong because it contradicts one of those
sentences, and that's how I treat each one below.

## How I ran the audit

My script requested every URL listed in the sitemaps of four sites and this
one, once each, without following redirects. It recorded each page's status,
robots directives, canonical, and dates. Then it compared the internal links
on the pages it read with what the sitemaps list.

I identify the sites only by code, as the [measurement
summary](https://github.com/Alf4kill/seotecnico.dev.br/blob/main/audits/case-studies/sitemap-audit-2026-11-11/summary.json)
does. I round totals and give small counts as ranges. In the table, "ok"
means a 200 status, no `noindex`, and a canonical that's either absent or points
to the URL itself.

## The checklist, with one case per item

Each item below has a case from the measurement, the Google sentence it
contradicts, and a fix. Some also explain why the item may not matter.

### 1. A `noindex` page in the sitemap

**Case.** On S3, one of the index's child sitemaps lists about 80 URLs, and
every one of them carries `noindex`. That file exists only to list pages the
site itself doesn't want indexed, and its URLs are 11% of everything S3's
sitemaps list. On S4, a file of landing pages and forms has `noindex` on 17% of
its URLs.

**What it contradicts.** Google asks you to list the URLs you want to see in
search results. Search Console's Page indexing report also says a spike in
errors can come from a sitemap that lists URLs blocked by robots.txt, marked
`noindex`, or behind a login.

**Fix.** Have your sitemap generator filter on the same field that sets the
`noindex`. If no item of a content type should be indexed, drop the whole type
from the sitemap instead of removing items one by one. Don't do the opposite
and block those URLs in robots.txt. As Google's `noindex` page points out, the
crawler has to open the page to see the rule.

### 2. A URL that redirects

**Case.** On S3, 5–9 listed author pages redirect to the home page. On S4,
about 40 redirects are spread across its three URL files. On S2, one real
estate ad in the sitemap redirects to a category page.

**What it contradicts.** Google tries to crawl each URL "exactly as listed".
If the URL redirects, the sitemap points to an address the site has already
replaced with another one.

**Fix.** List the destination instead, or list nothing if it's the home page.
Google's site move guide says that redirecting many URLs to an
irrelevant destination, such as the home page, might be treated as a [soft
404](/en/blog/soft-404).

Crawl budget isn't a reason to fix this. Google's crawl budget guide, last
updated July 22, 2026, is written for sites with about a million pages, or ten
thousand that change every day. For any other site, keeping the sitemap up to
date is enough.

### 3. A canonical that points to another URL

**Case.** On S4, about 30 listed URLs declare a different URL as canonical.
Most of them are in the same file of landing pages and forms.

**What it contradicts.** This breaks the documentation's most direct
sentence, which asks you not to name one URL in the sitemap and another in the
same page's `rel="canonical"`. Because the sitemap is the weaker signal, the
canonical should win, but the site still sends two opposite signals.

**Fix.** List the URL that the page declares as canonical, never the variant.

### 4. A URL that answers 404

**Case.** On S3, two FAQ sitemaps each list one URL that answers 404. On S4,
1–4 URLs in the landing pages file do.

**What it contradicts.** The Page indexing report says to fix only the 404s
you link to or that are in your sitemap. A listed 404 is, by definition, one
you pointed to yourself.

**Fix.** Remove the URL from the sitemap, or bring the page back.

### 5. Repeated entries and malformed URLs

**Case.** On S4, about 20 URLs appear in two different files. On S2 and S3,
1–4 do. On S3, one URL has an encoded space in its path (`%20`), and the
page's canonical repeats that address.

**What it contradicts.** A repeated URL isn't a protocol error, and the
Sitemaps report counts it only once. What it reveals is two generators that
don't know about each other. The space is different, because it's a problem
with the address itself. The protocol requires URLs to be escaped for the
server, and a space isn't a valid character in a URL path.

**Fix.** Put each page type in a single file, and generate slugs without
spaces.

### 6. A `lastmod` that shows when the file was served

**Case.** This is the most instructive item, because both sides are on the
same site. On S4, the posts file gives each post its own `lastmod`, which
matches the modification date the page itself declares in 100% of the posts
that have one. The site's pages file gives all of its roughly 70 URLs the
*same* `lastmod`, milliseconds apart, equal to the second the file was served.
That date says when the file was built, not when the page changed.

**What it contradicts.** Google's page on building a sitemap says Google uses
`lastmod` when it's "consistently and verifiably" accurate, for example when you
compare it with the page's last modification. A Search Central post from June
26, 2023, goes further. In it, a `lastmod` that says "changed yesterday" on a
page nobody has touched in years eventually makes Google stop believing it.
The sitemaps.org protocol is explicit that the date refers to the page's
modification, not the sitemap's generation. In a post from February 1, 2023,
Bing called this the most common `lastmod` mistake it finds.

**Fix.** Use `lastmod` only where a real date exists, and leave it out where one
doesn't. Google's 2023 post says it's fine to leave `lastmod` off a home page or
a category page when your software can't tell when the page changed.

The bug belongs to each file's generator, not to the site as a whole, which is
why the audit looks at one file at a time.

### 7. An important page missing from the sitemap

**Case.** On S2, some city category pages are in the menu of *every* page on
the site. They answer 200, they're indexable, and no sitemap lists them, while
other city categories are listed. On S4, indexable pages linked from 94% of
the pages the script read don't appear in any sitemap, and neither does a tool
linked from about one page in five.

**What it contradicts.** Again, Google asks you to include the URLs you want
in search results. Judging by the site's own structure, a page in every other
page's menu is one it most wants to show.

**Why it may not matter.** Google's sitemap overview says that on a
well-linked site, Google usually discovers most pages even without a sitemap,
so these pages are probably found. What you lose is a sitemap you can trust as
a list of what matters. If you own the site, you also lose sight of these
pages in the Page indexing report's "submitted pages" filter, which leaves
them out.

No sitemap validator finds this item, because validators read the file. You
find it by comparing the file with the site's links.

### 8. An empty sitemap announced in robots.txt

**Case.** On S1, robots.txt points to a `sitemap.xml` that answers 200 with zero
URLs. The article on [one page per keyword](/en/blog/one-page-per-keyword)
has the full analysis of that site.

**What it contradicts.** It contradicts very little. For a small, fully linked
site, Google says a sitemap may not even be needed. An empty file doesn't hurt
discovery, but it doesn't help either. This is the lightest item on the list, and
it's here because it's the easiest to spot.

## What is not a problem

Some findings look like problems, and the documentation says they aren't.

| Finding | Why it is not a problem |
|---|---|
| Order of URLs in the file | Google says order does not matter |
| Identical `priority` and `changefreq` everywhere | Google ignores both, and Bing largely does too |
| The same index answering at three addresses (S3) | It is one index with three ways in, and no URL changes |
| Pages without `lastmod` | No date is better than an invented one |
| No sitemap on a small, fully linked site | Google says it may not be needed |
| Transient 500 errors (S4) | All 22 URLs that answered 500 returned 200 when requested again, less than an hour later |

If you audit sitemaps, keep in mind that a 5xx during a crawl can be
momentary and not the sitemap's fault. Request the URL again before you write
it down.

## How to audit your own sitemap

My script runs five steps in order. You can follow the same order by hand,
with a crawler like [the free version of Screaming
Frog](/en/blog/screaming-frog-free-version), or with the [script from this
measurement](https://github.com/Alf4kill/seotecnico.dev.br/blob/main/scripts/sitemap-audit.mjs).

1. **Find every sitemap.** Check the `Sitemap:` lines in robots.txt, the
   default paths (`/sitemap.xml`, `/sitemap_index.xml`), and each index's
   children.
2. **Request each listed URL without following redirects.** Note its status,
   robots rules (both the meta tag and the `X-Robots-Tag` header), and
   canonical.
3. **Split the results by file.** On S4, one file was almost perfect and
   another had a problem on half its URLs.
4. **Compare `lastmod` with the page's own dates** (`dateModified` in JSON-LD or
   `article:modified_time`). Compare the instant, not the day. S4 writes
   `lastmod` in UTC and the page's dates in Brasília time (UTC−3, Brazil's
   official time zone), so an evening post can land on different days in each
   even though it's the same moment. Comparing by day gave me a false 6%
   mismatch in my first count.
5. **Compare the internal links with the list.** Any page linked from most of
   the site but absent from the sitemap is item 7.

To check a single URL, use the [meta tag
validator](/en/tools/meta-tag-validator). It shows the HTTP status, robots
rules, and canonical that the URL returns. For a whole site, you run the
script with one command.

```bash
node scripts/sitemap-audit.mjs --targets alvos.json --out audits/case-studies/sitemap-audit-AAAA-MM-DD
node scripts/sitemap-audit.mjs --recheck-5xx S4 --out audits/case-studies/sitemap-audit-AAAA-MM-DD
```

For the targets file's format, see
[`sitemap-audit.example.json`](https://github.com/Alf4kill/seotecnico.dev.br/blob/main/audits/case-studies/sitemap-audit.example.json).
The script reads robots.txt first and respects what it blocks. It makes one
request per second, and it never requests URLs on another host, even when the
sitemap lists them.

## What Search Console would show

If you have access to the site's Search Console, you can see what an outside
audit can't.

- **The Sitemaps report** shows whether Google could read each file and how
  many URLs it discovered in it. It only lists sitemaps submitted through the
  report itself or the API. Sitemaps declared in robots.txt don't appear
  there.
- **The Page indexing report**, filtered to "All submitted pages", counts URLs
  from sitemaps that were submitted or declared in robots.txt. It shows which
  ones are out of the index and why. That's where the checklist's `noindex` URLs
  and redirects show up as exclusions.

I didn't see any of the four sites through Search Console. I analyzed them
from the outside, and I didn't measure what Google indexed from each one.

## Keeping the sitemap clean in Next.js

This site served as the measurement's control. All 82 of its listed URLs were
ok, and no indexable page linked from almost every page is missing from the
sitemap. Only the RSS feed is linked everywhere and absent, and it isn't HTML.
Two decisions in the code explain this.

The first is about `lastmod`. `src/app/sitemap.ts` emits a date only when the
content provides one.

```ts
// src/app/sitemap.ts, linhas 13 e 14
// lastmod só é emitido quando derivável do frontmatter: um lastmod que muda a
// cada build sem mudança real de conteúdo ensina o Google a ignorá-lo.
```

```ts
// src/app/sitemap.ts, linhas 64 a 68
    // Home e "sobre" em inglês. Sem lastmod: nenhuma das duas tem frontmatter
    // de onde derivá-lo, e um lastmod inventado é o que ensina o Google a
    // ignorar a tag (mesma regra de /sobre e /ferramentas).
    { url: `${base}/en`, changeFrequency: 'monthly', priority: 0.8 },
    { url: `${base}/en/about`, changeFrequency: 'monthly', priority: 0.5 },
```

In the measurement, 11 of the 82 entries had no `lastmod`, and every other
entry's date matched the page's `dateModified`. The file still declares
`changeFrequency` and `priority`, which Google ignores. They don't hurt, but
they don't do anything either.

The second decision is structural. My SEO test suite runs on every pull
request and has no route list of its own. Its routes *are* the sitemap.

```ts
// tests/seo/seo.spec.ts, linhas 28 a 32
// Sitemap entries carry production-absolute URLs; tests run against localhost,
// so only the pathname is kept.
const sitemapPaths = sitemap().map((entry) => new URL(entry.url).pathname)
const noindexPaths = ['/busca', '/en/search']
const routes = [...sitemapPaths, ...noindexPaths]
```

For each route, the test requires a 200 status, a canonical equal to the
route's own URL, and no `noindex`. If this sitemap lists a `noindex` page, a URL
that redirects, or one that answers 404, CI fails before the deploy. A
redirect fails because its destination's canonical doesn't match the listed
URL. That way, items 1 to 4 of the checklist are tested without a single test
written for them.

To see how `sitemap.ts` is derived from content and what each field does in
Next.js, read [dynamic sitemaps in Next.js](/en/blog/nextjs-dynamic-sitemap).
My [technical SEO guide for Next.js](/en/guide/technical-seo-nextjs) covers
the rest, including metadata, canonicals, and redirects.

## Method and limits

- **Collection.** I measured this site and four public sites on November 11,
  2026, between 13:15 and 14:39 UTC, with the [audit
  script](https://github.com/Alf4kill/seotecnico.dev.br/blob/main/scripts/sitemap-audit.mjs).
  It uses a User-Agent that identifies it, respects robots.txt, and waits at
  least one second between requests. It sent about 4,200 requests in total. I
  didn't query any search engine.
- **Numbers.** I rounded the third-party numbers. Totals go to the nearest 10
  (the nearest 100 above 1,000), counts under ten appear as ranges, and shares
  are whole percentages. This site's numbers are exact. The
  [summary](https://github.com/Alf4kill/seotecnico.dev.br/blob/main/audits/case-studies/sitemap-audit-2026-11-11/summary.json)
  is the same file this article draws on.
- **Two corrections.** I fixed two mistakes during the measurement. One was
  comparing dates by day, which produced false mismatches across time zones.
  The other was counting pages without `dateModified` as having a different
  date. Both are in the script's history and in the [research
  note](https://github.com/Alf4kill/seotecnico.dev.br/blob/main/docs/research/2026-11-11-sitemap-audit.md).
- **A snapshot.** Sites regenerate their sitemaps all the time. In an earlier
  study of the same sites, four days before this one, S2 listed two
  redirects. This time it listed one.
- **Not measured.** I didn't measure what Google crawled and indexed from each
  site, because I analyzed them from the outside. I also didn't check whether
  a correct modification date matches a significant content change, which you
  can't see from the outside.

I analyzed the four public sites from outside, only to study the method. I
don't identify them, I have no relationship with them, and they didn't take
part. This isn't an audit, a recommendation to their owners, or a verdict on
them. Nobody sponsored it, and I don't sell any service from it.
