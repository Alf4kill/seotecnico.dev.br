Index bloat is the SEO industry's name for an index that holds many pages
from one site that are worth little to searchers. They're usually URLs the CMS
creates on its own, like tag archives, pagination, and one URL per small item.

The site I measured has those ingredients, even though its posts are long and
original. Nearly 4 in 10 of the URLs it offers for indexing are tag archives
that list one post at the median, pages with a single FAQ question, and pages
with a single testimonial.

The term comes from the industry, not from Google, and it's about the index.
From the outside, without Search Console, I can only measure what the site
offers. If Google indexed a good share of those URLs, the site has index bloat
in the term's sense. Common definitions attribute some effects to it. They get
their own section below, each next to what Google's documentation says about
it.

My [one page per keyword](/en/blog/one-page-per-keyword) article covered the
opposite case. Its pages' text was the problem, and that text still passed any
duplicate checker. Here, the posts' text isn't what's wrong. What's left is
everything that a CMS, a theme, and three subdomains offer around it. I don't
identify the site, and I didn't use search results. You'll find what the
server sent, what Google's documentation says, and each piece's probable
outcome.

## The site and how I measured it

It's a WordPress site with a blog of about 330 posts, some service pages, and
a sitemap index that a plugin generates. In my articles on [auditing a
sitemap](/en/blog/sitemap-audit) and [raw versus rendered
HTML](/en/blog/raw-vs-rendered-html), I called it "S3". Here it appears in
full.

I measured it with a [script in this
repository](https://github.com/Alf4kill/seotecnico.dev.br/blob/main/scripts/index-inventory.mjs).
It opens the sitemaps, requests each listed URL once, and measures what each
page type contains. It sends at most one request per second, reads
`robots.txt` first, and identifies itself in its User-Agent. It reads raw HTML
only, runs no JavaScript, and doesn't search Google. I ran it on November 17,
2026 (UTC).

Two definitions shape the numbers below. I count a URL as indexable when it
answers 200 without a redirect, has no `noindex`, and either has a canonical
that points to itself or has no canonical at all. Own words are the words in
text blocks that don't repeat on 30% or more of the pages I read. That leaves
out the menu, footer, "most read" box, and fixed call to action, because they
appear on almost every page.

I rounded the numbers for the site I analyzed, because an exact value helps
identify a site. As a control, I ran the same script on the same night on this
site's sitemap, and I give its numbers exactly. The control shows what the
method returns on a site without these page types.

## When every tag, question, and testimonial gets its own URL

After posts, tags are the largest group. At the median, each tag archive lists
one post, and more than 9 in 10 list one post or none. A tag's own content is
its name, its category, and that post's title, about 20 words in all. So a tag
archive is a second URL, with less text, for a post that already has its own.

WordPress creates these pages on its own. According to [its
documentation](https://wordpress.org/documentation/article/posts-tags-screen/),
when you click a tag, you get a page with every post that uses it. The
documentation doesn't mention indexing, which is left to the theme and SEO
plugin. On the analyzed site, every tag is indexable and in the sitemap.

Almost nothing links to them. Across all the pages I read, I counted which
pages link to each tag, leaving out each tag's link to itself. Only a few tags
get a link from another page. Posts don't link to their own tags, and the rest
appear only in the sitemap.

FAQs and testimonials follow the same pattern. Each FAQ URL holds one question
and its answer, about 50 own words, and almost none of them gets a link from
another page I read. Each testimonial has its own URL with about 40 words, and
no other page links to any of them. Between 1 and 4 of the FAQ URLs listed in
the sitemap answer 404.

All of this can be bad, and "can" is as far as I can go, because word count
decides nothing. [Google's SEO Starter
Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide)
says content length alone doesn't matter for ranking, and that there's no
minimum or maximum word count. Its [helpful content
guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)
asks whether text was written to hit a word count that Google prefers, and
answers that Google has none. So I don't call any of these pages thin for
having few words.

What the documentation does support is an argument about the whole site. When
a drop is site-wide, Google's [core updates
page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-updates) tells
you to evaluate the site as a whole, not just individual pages. It treats
deleting content as a last resort, but adds that if whole sections were
created for search engines first, deleting the unhelpful content can help the
site's good content. [Google's page experience
text](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/page-experience)
says its systems generally evaluate content page by page, but that some
assessments are site-wide.

If they serve no one who searches, one-post tags, one URL per question, and
one per testimonial are the kind of section those pages describe. That's a
documented possibility, with no number and no guaranteed effect. It's also the
only one I can support from the outside.

## Every page carries the same description

On every page, the theme prints the same `<meta name="description">`, a fixed
sentence about the company. On posts and partner landing pages, the SEO plugin
prints each page's own description first, and the theme's follows. So almost
all posts and all partner landing pages end up with two description tags.
Tags, FAQs, testimonials, categories, and author archives get only the fixed
sentence.

[Google's snippet
page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/snippet) says
plainly why that's bad. Identical or similar descriptions on every page aren't
helpful when pages show up in results, and it asks you to describe each
specific page. It also makes an exception. Site-level descriptions are fine on
the home page and on aggregation pages, and a tag archive is one of those. By
the documentation, then, the fixed sentence is acceptable on tags, but not on
FAQs and testimonials, which are individual pages.

The fixed sentence affects snippets, not indexing. Google builds snippets
mainly from page content, and sometimes uses the description when it
describes the page better than the content does. A sentence repeated across
about 630 URLs describes none of them, so it changes what Google has to build
a snippet from. It doesn't change whether a page gets indexed.

On posts, the second tag is the problem. Nowhere in the Google pages I
consulted did I find which of two description tags Google uses, and I didn't
test it, so its effect stays unmeasured. My control has no repeated
description.

No category archive has an H1. Google's SEO guide says the number and order of
your headings don't matter for Search, so this affects screen-reader users,
not the index. Enter a URL in the [meta tag
validator](/en/tools/meta-tag-validator) and it shows the page's title, first
description, H1 count, and status. You'll see a second description tag only in
the page source.

## Three subdomains and the status each one should return

Every HTTP response carries a status code that tells a crawler what happened
to the URL it requested. It says the URL exists (200), moved (301), or doesn't exist (404),
or that the server failed (5xx). [Google's JavaScript SEO
guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/javascript-seo-basics)
asks for a meaningful status, like 404 for a page that wasn't found. A
response is wrong when its status says one thing and the situation is another.

The main site gets this right and answers 404 for an invented URL. The three
subdomains it links to don't. On the same day, I probed each one with an
invented route, `/robots.txt`, and `/sitemap.xml`.

| Host | Root | Invented route | `/robots.txt` | Linked from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Old blog | 526 | 526 | 526 | the home page and a little over 1 in 10 pages read |
| App (client-rendered SPA) | 200 | 200, same HTML as the root | 406 or the app's HTML, depending on `Accept` | every page read |
| Subdomain run by a partner | 200 | 301 to `/404`, which answers 200 without `noindex` | 200, text | few pages read, not the home page |

### The old blog answers 526 on every path

According to [Cloudflare's
documentation](https://developers.cloudflare.com/support/troubleshooting/http-status-codes/cloudflare-5xx-errors/error-526/),
526 means Cloudflare couldn't validate the origin server's SSL certificate.
It's a configuration fault, not a decision about content, and it applies to
the whole host, `robots.txt` included.

A 526 doesn't say what happened to the blog, and that's the problem.
According to [Google's HTTP status
documentation](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/troubleshooting/http-status-codes),
a 5xx slows the crawler down, and Google keeps URLs already in the index for a
while, until they drop out. Under [the robots.txt
specification](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/robots-txt/robots-txt-spec),
if `robots.txt` returns 5xx, Google stops crawling the host for the first 12
hours. After 30 days, if the host still has availability problems, Google
stops crawling it.

What the host should answer depends on what happened. If the content moved,
[Google's site move
guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes)
asks for a permanent redirect (301 or 308) from each old URL to its new one.
If it was removed, the right answer is 404 or 410. Either one tells Google
what to do with each URL, while 526 only says that something broke.

The main site's home page still links to this host. For 404s, the [Search
Console Page indexing
report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7440203?hl=en) recommends
fixing the errors that your own site links to or lists in a sitemap. The same
reasoning applies to a whole host that won't open and that the home page
links to. Whether this affects the main host's ranking isn't documented,
because the [crawl budget
guide](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawl-budget) treats each
hostname as a separate site.

### The app answers 200 for any route

For any route, including one that doesn't exist, the app answers 200 with the
same short HTML. So to a crawler, any invented URL that someone links to is a
valid page showing the app's start screen. Google's JavaScript guide covers
this case by name. For client-routed SPAs, it gives two ways to avoid soft
404s: a JavaScript redirect to a URL the server answers with 404, or a
`noindex` that JavaScript adds to the error screen.

The app's `/robots.txt` has a different problem. It answers 406 when the
request accepts only text, and sends the app's HTML when the request accepts
anything. The specification asks for a UTF-8 plain-text file, and it covers
both cases. Google treats a 4xx as if no file existed, so nothing restricts
crawling. From HTML, Google parses whatever valid lines it finds and ignores
the rest. Either way, the app has no rules.

That matters only if the owner expected rules on this host or wants the app's
screens out of Search. Every page I read on the main site links to the app, so
Google reaches it. To keep those screens out of Search, Google documents
[`noindex`](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/block-indexing),
not `robots.txt`.

### The partner subdomain's 404 page answers 200

On this host, an invented route gets a 301 to `/404`, and `/404` answers 200
without `noindex`. The Page indexing report calls that a soft 404, a "not
found" message without the 404 code, and recommends that you return 404 for
pages that truly don't exist.

This redirect chain misleads in a specific way. Google treats a 301 as a
strong signal that it should process the target, and here the target answers
200. The site move guide warns that sending many old URLs to one unrelated
page may be treated as a soft 404, and the crawl budget guide notes that soft
404 pages keep getting crawled. On a small host, the extra crawling matters
little. The signal is what's wrong, because Google has to infer from the
content what the status should have said.

On November 9, this host answered 403 to my probe, which identifies itself as
this site's in its User-Agent. On November 17, it answered normally, so I
could measure the problem directly. My [soft 404 article](/en/blog/soft-404)
covers the other kinds.

## How fast the server responds, and Core Web Vitals in the field

The server is slow. In my run, the median time to first byte was about 1.5 s
on posts and about 2 s on the home page, against 339 ms for the control. That
was one machine, in Brazil, on one night, so it describes the run, not users.

For users, you need CrUX, which reports each origin's 75th percentile over 28
days, here October 18 to November 14, 2026. I rounded the values, because an
origin's exact p75 on a given date is enough to identify it.

|  | LCP | INP | CLS | TTFB |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phone | about 4 s, needs improvement | about 250 ms, needs improvement | about 0.2, needs improvement | about 3 s, poor |
| Desktop | about 3.5 s, needs improvement | about 100 ms, good | about 0.1, good | about 2.5 s, poor |

[Google's Core Web Vitals
guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals)
puts "good" at 2.5 s for LCP, 200 ms for INP, and 0.1 for CLS. Its [page
experience
page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/page-experience)
says ranking systems use these metrics, that there's no single page experience
signal, and that good results don't guarantee ranking. TTFB isn't a Core Web
Vital, but [web.dev's guide](https://web.dev/articles/ttfb) rates it poor
above 1.8 s, and everything the page draws comes after it. You can query the
same CrUX data for any origin with the [Core Web Vitals
checker](/en/tools/cwv-checker).

## What common definitions of index bloat claim

The [Ahrefs glossary](https://ahrefs.com/seo/glossary/index-bloat) defines
index bloat as an index holding too many pages from one site, many of them of
no value to users. It says this can waste crawl budget and affect rankings.
The term doesn't appear in the Google pages I consulted. Google's
documentation treats each of those effects on its own terms, and not always in
the definition's favor.

| The common definition says index bloat | What Google's documentation says | In this case |
|---|---|---|
| Wastes crawl budget | The [crawl budget guide](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawl-budget) is for sites with 1 million+ pages that change weekly, 10,000+ pages that change daily, or a large share of URLs in "Discovered – currently not indexed". It calls these numbers rough estimates | About 630 indexable URLs. Only Search Console shows the third criterion, but at this size crawling is not the probable problem |
| Affects rankings | The [core updates page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-updates) says to look at the site as a whole, and that deleting unhelpful content made for search engines can help the good content | Possible, with no number. One-post tags, one-question FAQs, and stand-alone testimonials are the kind of section that page describes |
| Makes search engines display pages badly | The [snippet page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/snippet) says identical descriptions on every page are not helpful | Measured. The fixed sentence does not describe the FAQs or the testimonials |

So I use the term to name the symptom, and Google's documentation for its
effects. Here I measured only the third effect, and the first doesn't apply
to a site this size.

## What I would do if it were my site

These recommendations are conditional. Before acting on any of them, I'd check
the site's Search Console to see which of these URLs have impressions. I'd
keep those until there's a reason to remove them.

- **Take one-post tags out of the index.** If a tag gathers fewer than two
  posts, give it `noindex` and take it out of the sitemap, or merge it into
  another tag. Tags that cover a real topic stay.
- **Turn the FAQ into one page.** The questions become sections of a single
  FAQ page, and the old URLs redirect to the right section.
- **Put each testimonial where it persuades.** Each one goes on the service
  page it's about, and the testimonials' own URLs go away.
- **Give each page one description.** The theme stops printing the fixed
  sentence on posts, FAQs, and testimonials. The plugin's description stays,
  and pages without one get one.
- **Make each host answer for itself.** The old blog gets a valid certificate
  and a redirect to the current blog, or answers 404 or 410, and the home page
  stops linking to it. The app sends nonexistent routes to a URL that answers
  404, as the JavaScript guide says. The partner returns a real 404 for what
  doesn't exist.

A `noindex` removes a URL from the index but doesn't save you any crawling,
because Google still requests the page. If you want Google to crawl less, the FAQ on this
page covers what the crawl budget guide points to.

## Why this Next.js site has no taxonomy archives

My [technical SEO guide for Next.js](/en/guide/technical-seo-nextjs) uses this
site as its reference implementation, and this site is also the control above.
After posts, taxonomy archives are the page type that weighs most on the
analyzed site, and this site has none. That was a decision, and the code
records why.

```ts
// src/lib/categories.ts, linhas 8 a 10
// Categorias NÃO geram URL. Com 12 artigos em 4 eixos, uma página de arquivo
// por categoria seria uma lista de três links — página fina, o risco que o §1
// nomeia. Elas servem ao filtro da /blog (sem JavaScript) e aos relacionados.
```

The comment says categories don't get a URL. With 12 articles across 4 topics,
a category archive would be a list of three links, a thin page in the
comment's words. Categories feed the `/blog` filter, which works without
JavaScript, and the related posts.

That comment dates from when the blog had 12 articles. Counting this one, the
blog has 29 across four categories, and the smallest has 3 articles. Its
archive would still be a list of three links.

On the blog page, a filter does the archive's job with radio buttons and CSS
`:has()`. It needs no JavaScript and no new URL.

```css
/* src/app/globals.css, linhas 397 a 398 e 401 a 410 */
```

Its comment adds that no category page is indexable, and that the decision is
recorded in `docs/design-system.md`.

Because no category has a route, the sitemap has nothing to list beyond
articles and written pages. The same reasoning applies to any
`generateStaticParams`. Each value it returns becomes an indexable URL, so for
each one, ask whether it gets a page that stands on its own. [Dynamic sitemap
in Next.js](/en/blog/nextjs-dynamic-sitemap) shows how this site's sitemap
comes from the same content.

## How to check your own site

1. List your sitemap URLs by type, using the name of each child sitemap. For
   each type, count how many answer 200, have no `noindex`, and carry their
   own canonical.
2. For each type, count the words that don't repeat on other pages. Look first
   at any whole type that has only a few dozen words.
3. Count the `description` tags on each page. Two tags mean your theme and
   plugin each print one. The same sentence on every page means a fixed
   description.
4. Request an invented URL on every host you own, including app and partner
   subdomains, and request each host's `/robots.txt`.
5. In Search Console, open the Page indexing report and see which status each
   type falls into. For pages like these, look for "Crawled – currently not
   indexed", "Duplicate without user-selected canonical", and "Soft 404". This
   is where you confirm whether there's index bloat in the term's sense.

This article's script covers steps 1 to 3 with its `fetch` and `analyze`
phases. Step 4 is its `probe` phase, which runs when the targets file lists
the hosts to probe.

```bash
node scripts/index-inventory.mjs fetch   --targets audits/case-studies/index-inventory.example.json --only SITE
node scripts/index-inventory.mjs analyze --targets audits/case-studies/index-inventory.example.json --only SITE
```

## Method and limits

- **Collection.** I ran the round on November 17, 2026 (the UTC date, which
  was the evening of November 16 in Brasília time, UTC−3). The script
  identified itself in its own User-Agent, respected `robots.txt`, and sent at
  most one request per second. The [versioned
  summary](https://github.com/Alf4kill/seotecnico.dev.br/blob/main/audits/case-studies/index-inventory-2026-11-17/summary.json)
  holds only codes and numbers. I don't publish addresses, the downloaded
  HTML, or the site's sentences.
- **Rounding.** So the site can't be identified, I rounded its totals to the
  nearest ten and large medians to the nearest hundred. Small counts appear in
  bands, and shares appear in words or as approximate proportions. I rounded
  the CrUX values too, because anyone can look up an origin's exact p75. The
  control's numbers are exact. The control isn't in CrUX, because it has too
  few visitors.
- **Definitions.** Own words are the words in text blocks that appear on fewer
  than 30% of the pages I read. I checked this by hand on one tag and one FAQ.
  On the tag, what's left is the tag's name and category, the post title, and
  a label from the tag template. On the FAQ, it's the question and its answer.
  A block repeated only within one type, like that label, counts as own text
  when the whole type is under 30% of the pages. If anything, the tags' count
  errs high. A tag's listed posts are its content links to URLs in the posts
  sitemap, not counting links repeated across the site.
- **Not measured.** What's indexed, clicks, rankings, and manual actions,
  because I analyzed the site from the outside. Without the index, this
  article doesn't claim the site has index bloat, only that it has the
  ingredients. I ran no Google searches for this article, and I didn't test
  what effect the double description has.
- **Correction.** An earlier, unpublished pass over this site, from November
  7, 2026, counted tags and FAQs as pages without a description. It checked
  the plugin's field, but the theme's fixed sentence was there.
- **Rendering.** The script runs no JavaScript. The comparison with rendered
  HTML comes from the November 13, 2026 run.

I analyzed this public site from the outside, only to study the method. I
don't identify it, I have no relationship with it, and it didn't take part.
This isn't an audit or a verdict on it, and the recommendations here are
general and conditional. Nobody sponsored this analysis, and I don't sell any
service based on it.
